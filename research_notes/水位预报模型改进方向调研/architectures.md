# Sequence-model architectures as alternatives or successors to the LSTM for 1-6 h water-level / streamflow forecasting at gauges

Scope note. State of knowledge as of November 2026. Almost all large-sample head-to-head evidence is at DAILY resolution and for streamflow, not hourly water level; every finding below is labelled daily or hourly. Sources older than 2022 are marked "(pre-2022)". Where a number was not available in the text I could read, that is stated; nothing is estimated.

## 1. Transformer-family models (TFT, Informer, Autoformer, PatchTST, iTransformer, Crossformer, TimesNet, TimeXer): what do head-to-head comparisons against the LSTM show?

### Takeaway
In the most rigorous multi-basin benchmarks (all daily), no Transformer variant beats a well-tuned LSTM on short-lead forecasting with meteorological inputs plus lagged observations; the LSTM is ahead by about 0.07-0.11 KGE in simulation and about 0.08 KGE at 1-step-ahead forecasting, and the Temporal Fusion Transformer (which itself contains an LSTM) is a statistical tie (median NSE 0.821 vs 0.820). Transformers gain only in settings unlike this one: long horizons (7-60 steps) without future forcings, spatial extrapolation, or zero-shot.

### Cited Findings
- (DAILY, 531 CAMELS basins + 3,434 global basins) Liu, Shen et al., "From RNNs to Transformers", HESS 29, 6811-6828, published 1 Dec 2025, benchmarked 11 Transformer variants (CARDformer, Crossformer, ETSformer, Informer, iTransformer, Non-stationary Transformer, Pyraformer, Reformer, vanilla Transformer, PatchTST, TimesNet) plus DLinear against an LSTM. Simulation ("regression") median KGE: LSTM 0.80 on CAMELS and 0.75 on the global set; the best attention model was 0.07 lower on CAMELS (Crossformer) and 0.11 lower on the global set (Informer). The LSTM's median unbiased RMSE was 11.3% (CAMELS) and 12.0% (global) lower than the best attention model. — [Liu et al. 2025, HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/6811/2025/)
- (DAILY, same paper) Forecasting with meteorological inputs plus lagged observations, the setting closest to an operational gauge forecast: at 1-day lead LSTM KGE 0.89 vs 0.81 for the best attention model (ETSformer), about 0.08 lower; at 30-day lead the gap narrows to about 0.01. — [Liu et al. 2025, HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/6811/2025/)
- (DAILY, same paper) Autoregression from past observations only (no forcings): at 1 day Pyraformer 0.65 vs LSTM 0.64; at 7 days Pyraformer 0.32 vs LSTM 0.15. Attention models only pull ahead when there are no exogenous drivers and the horizon is long. — [Liu et al. 2025, HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/6811/2025/)
- (DAILY, same paper) Spatial hold-out on CAMELS: LSTM median KGE falls 0.80 to 0.62, Crossformer 0.73 to 0.63 (slightly above the LSTM). PatchTST and TimesNet were run only for the autoregression task "because training time grew sharply with basin count". The authors' conclusion: LSTM generally best in regression and short-term forecasting; attention models better for extreme flows and long-horizon autoregression. — [Liu et al. 2025, HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/6811/2025/)
- (DAILY, CAMELS) Liu, Bian, Lawson, Shen, Journal of Hydrology 637, 131389 (2024): a vanilla Transformer "is not competitive against LSTM" on CAMELS and lags "especially prominently for the high-flow metrics"; a recurrence-free modified Transformer gives "very slightly higher" KGE with mixed results on other metrics. The authors suggest state-of-the-art models may be close to the predictability limit of the dataset. The accessible page gives no numeric values. — [Liu et al. 2024, J. Hydrol. (NSF PAR record)](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10541025)
- (DAILY, 531 CAMELS basins, ensembles of 10 seeds each) Koya and Roy, "Efficacy of Temporal Fusion Transformers for Runoff Simulation" (arXiv, June 2025): median test NSE TFT 0.821 vs LSTM 0.820; KGE 0.801 vs 0.796; peak-flow bias FHV -12.9 vs -14.6; peak timing error 0.238 for both; Pearson r 0.915 for both. Authors: TFT "slightly outperforms" but the gap is not significant. On five Caravan subsets the two alternate (e.g. Australia KGE 0.55 TFT vs 0.61 LSTM; US NSE 0.56 vs 0.54). — [Koya and Roy 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.20831v1)
- (DAILY, 2,610 Caravan basins) The same group's earlier preprint (2023) claimed TFT outperforms LSTM and a Transformer; the 2025 follow-up with seed ensembles is the more careful test and shrinks the claim to a tie. — [Koya and Roy 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.12335); [Koya and Roy 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.20831v1)
- (HOURLY, 601 CAMELS basins, target = National Water Model retrospective streamflow one hour ahead, not observations) Akinrele et al. (arXiv, June 2026): 32-unit LSTM vs single-layer encoder-only Transformer, 256-step input. With downstream discharge added: median normalized NSE LSTM 0.93 vs Transformer 0.90; RMSE 0.44 vs 0.56; KGE 0.79 vs 0.80. "LSTM performed modestly but consistently better"; adding the downstream information mattered far more than the architecture (normalized NSE 0.56 to 0.90-0.93 for both). Neither model was exhaustively tuned. — [Akinrele et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.02791)
- (DAILY, CAMELS-US 671 catchments, spatial validation, no per-model tuning) He et al. (arXiv 2410.15218v3, Sept 2025): streamflow RMSE (normalized units) multivariate LSTM 0.0161, iTransformer 0.0232, PatchTST 0.0235, TSMixer 0.0255, TFT 0.2385, TimesNet 0.2943. The TFT and TimesNet values are an order of magnitude worse than the others, which suggests configuration problems rather than an intrinsic ranking; the authors state they did not tune hyperparameters per model. — [He et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.15218v3)
- (General time series, not hydrology) Zeng et al., "Are Transformers Effective for Time Series Forecasting?" (arXiv May 2022, later AAAI 2023): one-layer linear models (DLinear / NLinear) beat the Transformer long-horizon forecasters of the time on nine benchmark datasets "in all cases, often by a large margin"; argued permutation-invariant self-attention loses temporal ordering information. — [Zeng et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13504)
- (DAILY water level, 5 Everglades stations, 7-28 day lead, LSTM NOT among baselines) Rangaraj et al. (arXiv 2505.01415v2, Aug 2025): among 12 task-trained models, N-BEATS and PatchTST were best at 7 days (MAE 0.076 for both); iTransformer, Informer, TSMixer, DLinear/NLinear behind. — [Rangaraj et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.01415v2)

### Inferences
- For the task here (short lead, future exogenous covariates known, autoregressive use of the last observation, hundreds of gauges), the directly relevant row of the HESS benchmark is "forecasting with forcings plus lagged observations at the shortest lead", and there every Transformer variant lost to the LSTM by a wide margin (0.08 KGE). Nothing in the Transformer literature predicts a gain above the 1% (0.1 cm) threshold from swapping the LSTM for PatchTST / iTransformer / Crossformer / Informer / Autoformer / TimesNet.
- The TFT is the only Transformer-family model with repeated evidence of matching the LSTM, and it does so while containing an LSTM encoder-decoder; its measurable differences are in peak-flow bias (about 1.7 percentage points of FHV at daily scale), not in timing (peak-timing error identical). It is therefore unlikely to cure a 2.5 h lag on rising water.
- The repeated finding that Transformers benefit from longer inputs while the LSTM is flat (Koya and Roy 2025) is consistent with the null result already obtained here for 168 / 336 / 720 h look-backs: the LSTM does not need the longer window, so a model whose advantage is using long windows has little to offer.

### Gaps
- TimeXer: I found no hydrology or water-level study testing TimeXer against an LSTM. Its design (endogenous patches plus exogenous variate tokens) fits the problem on paper, but there is no evidence either way.
- Autoformer and Informer on hourly gauge forecasting with future covariates: no large-sample study found; the only large-sample Informer result is daily (HESS benchmark, 0.11 KGE below LSTM on the global set).
- The Journal of Hydrology 2024 Transformer paper's numeric tables were not accessible; only the qualitative abstract is reported above.
- I found no head-to-head on tide gauges at many stations with PatchTST / iTransformer vs LSTM. A conference poster describes a 217-station NOAA tide-gauge benchmark of LSTM variants, but I could not read its results. — [SC25 poster listing](https://sc25.supercomputing.org/proceedings/posters/poster_files/post277s2-file3.pdf)

## 2. Non-attention models: TCN, N-BEATS / N-HiTS, TiDE, DLinear, TSMixer, Mamba / state-space, xLSTM, GRU, encoder-decoder and "handoff" LSTMs, multi-timescale LSTM

### Takeaway
The credible large-sample evidence for a non-LSTM backbone beating an LSTM is limited to one Mamba model on a global daily grid with river-network context (KGE 0.661 vs 0.614 at gauges), which is a spatial model rather than a per-gauge sequence swap. N-HiTS / N-BEATS have a peer-reviewed hourly result (about 10% better at 1 h) but on two watersheds only. Encoder-decoder / handoff and multi-timescale LSTMs are engineering variants of the LSTM with no demonstrated accuracy gain at short lead; for xLSTM I found no published hydrology comparison at all.

### Cited Findings
- (HOURLY, 2 headwater watersheds in Georgia and North Carolina) Saberian and Samadi: N-HiTS improved hourly flood forecasting accuracy "by approximately 10% compared to LSTM and NWM" with negligible difference in computational cost, and forecast time-to-peak and peak rate more accurately (EGU 2024 abstract); the full paper (HESS 30, 371-399, 2026) reports both N-HiTS and N-BEATS outperform the LSTM for 1 h flood predictions. A referee asked for 3/6/12/24 h results and the authors narrowed the conclusion to the two watersheds. — [EGU24-13353](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU24/EGU24-13353.html); [HESS 30, 371, 2026](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/371/2026/)
- (DAILY, global 0.05 degree grid, 1-7 day lead, 3,366 GRDC gauges for evaluation) RiverMamba (NeurIPS 2025): bidirectional Mamba blocks over the river network with ECMWF forecasts as input. Against an encoder-decoder LSTM retrained on identical inputs: at GRDC gauges R2 0.506 vs 0.462, KGE 0.661 vs 0.614, flood F1 (1.5-20 yr return periods) 0.243 vs 0.148; on the reanalysis target KGE 0.913 vs 0.892, F1 0.459 vs 0.358. The gap to the LSTM is described as large for leads above 48 h. Pretraining took about 3 days on 16 A100 GPUs. The authors attribute the gain to spatio-temporal routing context that a per-point LSTM lacks. — [Shams Eddin et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22535v3)
- (531 CAMELS basins; resolution not stated on the abstract page) A frequency-tuned diagonal state-space model (S4D-FT) is reported to "outperform the LSTM model across diverse regions"; the abstract gives no numbers. Preprint, January 2025. — [Wang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14980)
- (DAILY, 531 CAMELS + global) DLinear was included as a baseline in the HESS architecture benchmark alongside the LSTM; the LSTM is reported as best for regression and short-term forecasting (DLinear-specific numbers were not in the text I could read). — [Liu et al. 2025, HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/6811/2025/)
- (DAILY, CAMELS-US 671, untuned) TSMixer streamflow RMSE 0.0255 vs multivariate LSTM 0.0161; the paper also lists TCN, DilatedRNN and TiDE among compared models, with the multivariate LSTM having the lowest RMSE of all. — [He et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.15218v3)
- (DAILY water level, Everglades, 28-day lead) TSMixer had the lowest RMSE among task-trained models (0.252) and N-BEATS the lowest MAE (0.176); the TSMixer variant with exogenous inputs (TSMixerx) scored best among task-trained models on an extreme-event index (0.659) but only average on overall error. No LSTM baseline. — [Rangaraj et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.01415v2)
- (DAILY, global, 5,680 gauges) The operational Google model (Nearing et al., Nature 2024) is an encoder-decoder LSTM: a hindcast LSTM over 365 days and a separate forecast LSTM over the 7-day horizon fed with weather forecasts; 256 cell states each; cell state passed linearly and hidden state through a tanh layer; output is an asymmetric-Laplacian distribution per lead time; three separately trained models are averaged; about 10 h training on one V100. The paper compares against the GloFAS physical model, not against other neural architectures, and uses no streamflow input. — [Nearing et al. 2024 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10954541/)
- NeuralHydrology ships four forecast-LSTM layouts: Handoff-Forecast-LSTM (hindcast LSTM states passed through a handoff network into a separate forecast LSTM), Multihead-Forecast-LSTM ("runs an LSTM up to the issue time, then predicts the whole forecast sequence directly with an FC layer, without recurrent rollout"), Sequential-Forecast-LSTM (one LSTM rolled through hindcast and forecast with separate embeddings) and Stacked-Forecast-LSTM. It also ships Transformer (encoder only), Mamba, xLSTM, GRU, MTS-LSTM, EA-LSTM, MC-LSTM, ARLSTM and ODE-LSTM classes. The docs give no accuracy ranking among them. — [NeuralHydrology model docs](https://neuralhydrology.readthedocs.io/en/latest/usage/models.html)
- (HOURLY simulation, 516 CAMELS basins; pre-2022) Gauch et al. 2021, MTS-LSTM: processes the distant past at daily steps and the recent past at hourly steps. A naive hourly LSTM's median NSE was only slightly lower than the multi-timescale models and the difference was "not significant in every comparison"; the benefit is cost (training about 6-8 h vs more than a day; inference about 13 min vs 9 h for 10 years at 516 basins, roughly 40x) and the ability to mix forcings of different resolution. Using multiple forcing products raised hourly median NSE from 0.776 to 0.812. LSTMs beat the National Water Model by about 0.19 median NSE at hourly scale. Evaluated in reanalysis mode, not as lead-time forecasts. — [Gauch et al. 2021, HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/25/2045/2021/)
- (HOURLY, 5 small fast catchments in Saxony; pre-2022, conference abstract) Morgenstern et al. tested four remedies for the phase-shift (lag) error of hourly LSTM flood forecasts: encoder-decoder LSTM and offsetting the forecast start (both structural) showed "no advantage"; reversing the input series and supplying observed future precipitation as a perfect forecast (both input changes) "show potential". No numbers given. — [EGU21-15072](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU21/EGU21-15072.html)
- (HOURLY, 1 basin of 17,100 km2 in China, 30 flood events, 1-6 h lead, 12 h input) An LSTM with feature-time multi-head attention and monotonicity penalties in the loss: NSE 0.988 vs 0.977 at 1 h and 0.908 vs 0.865 at 6 h against a plain LSTM (RMSE -27% and -21%). The authors say the lag "is mitigated to some extent" but remains, the model overshoots a single-peak event more than the others, and shows anomalous oscillations at 6 h in a double-peak event. Single-basin, event-based; it is unclear whether the reported comparison is on training or validation events. — [Zhang et al. 2025, HESS 29, 5955](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/5955/2025/)
- (HOURLY, 125 Iowa gauges, 120 h horizon, 72 h input) A generalized Transformer is reported to hold higher median NSE / KGE and the lowest normalized RMSE vs LSTM, GRU and Seq2Seq baselines (arXiv 2406.07484, 2024; numbers not readable in the sources I reached). The WaterBench-Iowa baselines on the same 125 watersheds had median NSE 0.74 and KGE 0.79 for the 120 h task. This is a 5-day horizon without the short-lead focus. — [Demiray and Demir 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07484); [WaterBench-Iowa, ESSD 2022](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/14/5605/2022/essd-14-5605-2022.pdf)
- (HOURLY water level, South Florida coastal canals) In one study RNN and LSTM did well at 1 h and 8 h leads but degraded faster at longer leads than MLP and CNN models, which were relatively insensitive to lead time. — [Shi et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.15907)
- (HOURLY, low confidence: search summaries only, attribution to the exact paper not verified) A TCN was reported better than an LSTM for hourly streamflow in one study area with a maximum useful lead of six hours (pre-2022); another study on two Spanish basins found adding future rainfall to the input improved results especially at longer leads. — [Hydrology Research 2020 record](https://doaj.org/article/19885750ccf9450c99d95b3d4eacaf6f); [Earth Science Informatics 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12145-024-01454-9)

### Inferences
- The existing model (LSTM run to the issue time, then all six leads predicted directly) is structurally the same as NeuralHydrology's Multihead-Forecast-LSTM. The handoff / sequential / stacked variants exist mainly to consume a forecast-period forcing sequence of many steps (7 daily steps in the Google model). With a horizon of only 6 steps and future covariates already given to a direct head, the literature offers no evidence that the handoff layout would be more accurate; the single test of encoder-decoder LSTMs against the lag problem reported no advantage.
- The RiverMamba gain (about +0.05 KGE at gauges) comes bundled with river-network spatial context and a gridded target. It is evidence that adding upstream / spatial information helps, more than evidence that replacing LSTM cells with Mamba cells helps at a single gauge. The existing model already takes upstream discharge as an input.
- N-HiTS / N-BEATS / TiDE / TSMixer are MLP models on a fixed window. They are cheap to try (minutes to hours of training) and the one hourly test favours N-HiTS at 1 h lead, but the evidence base is two watersheds. A realistic prior from the larger untuned comparisons (TSMixer 58% higher RMSE than the LSTM on CAMELS) is that they land near or below the LSTM once many gauges and exogenous inputs are involved.
- MTS-LSTM addresses cost of long hourly inputs. Since look-backs beyond 72 h gave nothing here, the multi-timescale design has nothing to recover.
- GRU: no source found claiming a systematic gain of GRU over LSTM at large sample; it appears as an equal-tier baseline (WaterBench).

### Gaps
- xLSTM: no peer-reviewed or preprint hydrology comparison of xLSTM vs LSTM was found (two targeted searches). It is available in NeuralHydrology and NeuralForecast, so the test is cheap, but there is no published expectation of the size of any gain.
- Mamba as a drop-in per-gauge sequence model (not a spatial model): no large-sample number found. A thesis mentions a bidirectional Mamba with spatiotemporal attention for hourly runoff (IEEE TKDE 2025) but I did not verify its results. — [UTS thesis](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/194411/1/thesis.pdf)
- TiDE and TCN at hourly resolution over many gauges with future covariates: no credible large-sample study found.
- WaveNet-style models: nothing specific found for 2022-2026 gauge forecasting.
- N-HiTS beyond two watersheds: the authors mentioned a planned test on additional USGS gauges; I could not confirm it was done.

## 3. Time-series foundation models (TimesFM, Chronos / Chronos-Bolt / Chronos-2, Moirai, TimeGPT, Lag-Llama, TTM, Timer, Sundial, hydrology-specific models)

### Takeaway
Zero-shot foundation models do not match a task-trained multi-basin LSTM that has meteorological inputs: on CAMELS the LSTM's streamflow RMSE is about 32-36% lower than Chronos-Bolt and Sundial, and the best zero-shot model in the one dedicated streamflow study is described only as "nearly as well as" the LSTM. They win in cases unlike this one (no local training data, univariate, long horizon, slowly varying wetland levels). Until late 2025 most could not take future covariates; Chronos-2 is the first widely used one that natively accepts past and known-future covariates, and I found no published test of it, or of any fine-tuned foundation model with rainfall covariates, against an LSTM on streamflow or water level.

### Cited Findings
- (DAILY, CAMELS-US, 534 train / 137 validation catchments) He et al. (arXiv 2511.11849v2, Nov 2025): validation RMSE for streamflow (normalized units): multivariate LSTM 0.0161, Chronos-Bolt-base zero-shot 0.0238, Sundial-base-128m zero-shot 0.0252. Both foundation models were run univariate-in, univariate-out without covariates. The LSTM wins on all six target variables. Conclusion: models with comprehensive known exogenous inputs outperform more limited approaches including foundation models. Caveat noted when reading: the paper is internally inconsistent about whether streamflow was among the LSTM's inputs. — [He et al. 2025b](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.11849v2)
- (DAILY, CAMELS-US 671, spatial validation) Chronos-T5 zero-shot streamflow RMSE 0.1403 vs multivariate LSTM 0.0161 and univariate LSTM 0.0190 (earlier, weaker Chronos generation). — [He et al. 2025a](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.15218v3)
- (More than 500 US basins; resolution not stated in the accessible summary) Sun and Sun, "Zero-shot Forecasting of Streamflow Using Time Series Foundation Models: Are We There Yet?", Machine Learning: Earth (2026): Sundial "performed nearly as well as" a fully trained LSTM; foundation models did best in basins with strong seasonal (snowmelt-driven) patterns, with "progress to be made, especially in more complex river systems". I could only reach the publisher's news item; no numbers. — [IOP Publishing news, 20 Mar 2026](https://ioppublishing.org/?p=170110)
- (DAILY, 7 basins only, zero-shot, univariate) In the HESS architecture benchmark, at 7-day horizon TimeGPT reached KGE 0.68 vs 0.50 for a supervised LSTM given the same past-observations-only input (GPT-3.5 0.53, Llama-3-8B 0.54); at 30 days TimeGPT 0.33 vs LSTM below 0.15. Lag-Llama and Tiny Time Mixers were run but only TimeGPT is reported in the text. TimeGPT "still missed peak flows"; the authors caution these models do not capture hydrologic relationships without domain adaptation. This comparison denies the LSTM its meteorological inputs. — [Liu et al. 2025, HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/6811/2025/)
- (DAILY water level, 5 Everglades stations, 7-28 day lead, zero-shot, no LSTM baseline) Chronos MAE / RMSE 0.049 / 0.075 at 7 days and 0.088 / 0.124 at 28 days, about 36% and 50% lower MAE than the best task-trained models (N-BEATS, PatchTST 0.076 at 7 days; N-BEATS 0.176 at 28 days). The other foundation models were much worse at 28 days: TimeGPT 0.238, TimesFM 0.342, Moirai 0.364, Timer 0.385 MAE. Extreme-event index at 28 days: Chronos 0.710, TimeGPT 0.150, TimesFM 0.058, Timer 0.000. Authors: all models struggle with extremes. — [Rangaraj et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.01415v2)
- Covariate support (capability table in the Chronos-2 report, Oct 2025): Chronos-2 supports univariate, multivariate, past-only covariates, known-future covariates and categorical covariates; TimesFM-2.5, Moirai-2.0, TiRex, Sundial and Chronos-Bolt support none (univariate only); TabPFN-TS supports known-future covariates but not past-only; COSMIC supports past and known covariates; Toto-1.0 supports multivariate and past-only covariates. Chronos-2 base has 120M parameters (small variant 28M), context up to 8,192 steps, and runs about 300 series per second on one A10G GPU. The report says in-context learning gives its largest gains on the covariate subset of its benchmark but gives no number in the text. — [Chronos-2 report](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15821v1)
- TimesFM 2.5 can use covariates only through an auxiliary linear regressor fitted at inference time on the context window (not inside the network), per a 2026 photovoltaic study; TiRex and Moirai 2.0 "remain univariate with respect to covariates". Secondary source. — [PV cold-start study 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.07457)
- The Darts library's current covariate table lists Chronos2Model, TimesFM3Model and TiRex2Model as accepting past and future covariates (no static covariates), and TimesFM2p5Model and TiRexModel as accepting none. This implies newer TimesFM and TiRex generations with covariate support exist by late 2026; I did not read their papers. — [Darts covariates guide](https://unit8co.github.io/darts/userguide/covariates.html)
- (General, not hydrology) Two 2025 conference papers caution on the foundation-model premise: zero-shot ability is tied to the pretraining domains and fine-tuned foundation models do not consistently beat smaller dedicated models on unseen real-world data; and a simple PCA + linear model can serve as an effective zero-shot forecaster. — [How Foundational are Foundation Models, 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.00742); [ICML 2025](https://icml.cc/virtual/2025/47480)
- Hydrology-side "foundation" work in 2026 is mostly large pretrained LSTMs that are then fine-tuned regionally (regionalised fine-tuning of a global LSTM improving NSE over the base model; a contrastive-pretrained LSTM fine-tuned beating locally trained models), not general time-series foundation models. — [EGUsphere 2026-2950](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-2950/egusphere-2026-2950.pdf); [EGUsphere 2026-1637](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-1637/egusphere-2026-1637.pdf)

### Inferences
- Every published foundation-model win over a neural baseline in hydrology is in a setting where the baseline lacks its usual advantages (7 basins with past-only input; daily wetland stages with no LSTM baseline). Where a multi-basin LSTM with forcings is the comparator, zero-shot models are 30-50% worse in RMSE or "nearly as good". A zero-shot swap would be expected to lose to a 13-year task-trained model at 1-6 h, particularly for wind-driven tide gauges and rain-driven urban creeks, where the signal is in the future covariates.
- The only foundation model whose interface matches this task (past-only covariates: past rain and upstream discharge; known-future covariates: forecast wind and rain) is Chronos-2 (and, per the Darts table, the newest TimesFM / TiRex generations). It would be the one to test, zero-shot and fine-tuned, but there is no published basis for expecting a gain.
- The Everglades result shows Chronos is strong on smooth, strongly autocorrelated water levels (lakes / wetlands). Lakes are already the best-behaved station type here (median 6 h RMSE 2.6 cm), so the place a foundation model is most likely to do well is the place with least to gain.

### Gaps
- No study found that fine-tunes Chronos-2, TimesFM, Moirai or TTM with precipitation / wind covariates and compares with a global LSTM on streamflow or water level (targeted search returned none).
- No foundation-model evaluation at hourly resolution with 1-12 h leads at gauges was found.
- The "Are We There Yet" paper's numbers (which models, how far behind the LSTM, resolution) were not accessible; only the publisher's summary.
- No general-purpose hydrology-specific time-series foundation model with published hourly gauge skill was found. A Google urban flash-flood system (2026) was described in press coverage as an LSTM trained on news-derived flood events giving a 24 h risk category on 20 km cells; I could not reach the primary paper, and it is not a gauge-level model. — [press coverage](https://decrypt.co/360946/googles-new-ai-tool-predicts-flash-floods-24-hours-advance)
- Lag-Llama and TTM: run in the HESS benchmark but results not reported in the text I could read.

## 4. Hourly and sub-daily evidence specifically (1-12 h leads)

### Takeaway
There is no standardized large-sample hourly benchmark that compares architectures at 1-12 h lead with future covariates. Hourly evidence is either LSTM-only at scale (516 CAMELS basins, about 1,600 German catchments), a simulated target (601 basins, LSTM slightly ahead of a Transformer), or one-to-five-basin studies with self-reported gains for the proposed model.

### Cited Findings
- (HOURLY, 601 basins, 1 h ahead, simulated target) LSTM normalized NSE 0.93 vs Transformer 0.90; RMSE 0.44 vs 0.56. — [Akinrele et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.02791)
- (HOURLY, 516 basins, simulation not forecasting; pre-2022) Hourly LSTMs with median NSE 0.776-0.812; naive hourly LSTM about equal to the multi-timescale variants in accuracy. — [Gauch et al. 2021](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/25/2045/2021/)
- (HOURLY, 2 watersheds, 1 h lead) N-HiTS and N-BEATS better than LSTM, about 10%. — [HESS 30, 371, 2026](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/371/2026/); [EGU24-13353](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU24/EGU24-13353.html)
- (HOURLY, 1 basin, 1-6 h lead) Attention-augmented, physics-penalized LSTM: NSE 0.908 vs 0.865 at 6 h; lag reduced but not removed. — [Zhang et al. 2025](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/5955/2025/)
- (HOURLY, 125 Iowa gauges, 120 h horizon) Transformer reported ahead of LSTM / GRU / Seq2Seq (numbers not accessed); dataset baselines median NSE 0.74. — [Demiray and Demir 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07484); [WaterBench-Iowa](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/14/5605/2022/essd-14-5605-2022.pdf)
- (HOURLY, 5 small catchments; pre-2022) Encoder-decoder LSTM gave no advantage against phase-shift error; changing the inputs did. — [EGU21-15072](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU21/EGU21-15072.html)
- New hourly large-sample datasets that could host such a benchmark: CAMELSH (9,008 US catchments, hourly streamflow for 3,166; Scientific Data 2025) and CAMELS-DE-1h (about 1,600 German catchments, 2001-2024, with archived 48 h ICON-D2 forecasts 2021-2024 and open LSTM benchmarks for simulation and forecasting). The CAMELS-DE-1h abstract reports no architecture comparison; a search summary of the dataset paper gives median NSE 0.82 for the LSTM vs 0.69 for the HBV conceptual model at 1,496 catchments. — [CAMELSH (PNNL record)](https://www.pnnl.gov/publications/camelsh-large-sample-hourly-hydrometeorological-dataset-and-attributes-watershed-scale); [EGU26-12386](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-12386.html); [ESSD preprint 2026-289](https://essd.copernicus.org/preprints/essd-2026-289/essd-2026-289.pdf)
- (HOURLY water level, tide gauges, Gulf of Mexico and Chesapeake Bay) An LSTM-based model with atmospheric inputs and astronomical tide met NOAA's operational criterion: 92.2% of predictions within 15 cm at Port Isabel and 90.4% at Rockport for leads up to 108 h. LSTM-only; no architecture comparison in the summary I saw. — [NSF PAR record](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10567247-exploring-deep-learning-methods-short-term-tide-gauge-water-level-predictions)
- A 2026 conference abstract describes an LSTM-based operational hourly flood forecasting approach designed to be robust to missing data; LSTM remains the operational default in 2026. — [EGU26-3180](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-3180.html)

### Inferences
- With about 443 real-time and 1,000 total gauges, hourly data from 2010, real forecast covariates and a seed-noise floor of 0.04 cm, the existing setup is a stronger test bed for architecture at 1-6 h than anything published. Any architecture conclusion will have to come from in-house experiments; the literature can only rank what is worth trying.
- The small hourly studies that report gains over a "plain LSTM" (10-27% RMSE) use per-basin or two-basin LSTM baselines of unknown tuning. The 2024 HESS opinion paper argues single-basin LSTM baselines systematically understate what an LSTM can do. Such gains should be discounted heavily when the comparator is a tuned multi-gauge LSTM ensemble. — [Kratzert et al. 2024, HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/4187/2024/)

### Gaps
- No architecture comparison found on CAMELSH or CAMELS-DE-1h as of November 2026.
- No large-sample hourly study found that includes hurricane or surge conditions, or that separates tide gauges, estuaries and urban creeks by architecture.
- No study found reporting lag (timing error in hours) by architecture at 6 h lead over many gauges; the only large-sample timing number is daily (TFT and LSTM identical).

## 5. Realistic expected gain from swapping the architecture alone; do inputs matter more?

### Takeaway
The weight of evidence says the expected gain from an architecture swap with inputs fixed is around zero, with a real chance of a loss: the best-documented alternative (TFT) is a tie (+0.001 NSE) and the mainstream Transformers are clearly worse at short lead. Papers that vary both consistently find inputs (extra forcing products, downstream / upstream discharge, static descriptors, future rainfall) move skill by far more than the architecture does.

### Cited Findings
- Architecture effect, best case: TFT vs LSTM median NSE 0.821 vs 0.820 (daily, 531 basins, 10-seed ensembles), "not significant". — [Koya and Roy 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.20831v1)
- Architecture effect, typical Transformer: 0.07-0.11 KGE below the LSTM in simulation; 0.08 below at 1-step forecasting. — [Liu et al. 2025, HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/6811/2025/)
- Input effect, same model: adding a second and third forcing product raised an hourly LSTM's median NSE from 0.776 to 0.812 (pre-2022). — [Gauch et al. 2021](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/25/2045/2021/)
- Input effect vs architecture effect in one study (hourly): adding downstream forcings and discharge moved normalized NSE from 0.56 to 0.93 (LSTM) and 0.56 to 0.90 (Transformer); the architecture difference was 0.03. Authors: downstream information "was the main driver of gains for both models, and it narrowed the performance gap across architectures". — [Akinrele et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.02791)
- Heudorfer, Gupta, Dolich, Loritz, "Better data or better architecture?" (HESS discussion paper, April 2026; daily, 531 CAMELS basins, ungauged cross-validation): compared a base LSTM, an embedding LSTM and a cross-attention LSTM across static-feature sets; conclude better static data matters more than better architecture, architectural changes are needed mainly to make use of better data, and "the single-layer LSTM remains the most suitable core model for now". Best configuration median NSE 0.726 (abstract) vs prior benchmark 0.69. Under review; referees flagged a 0.726 vs 0.721 inconsistency and some significance claims; only three LSTM-family architectures were compared. — [Heudorfer et al. 2026](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-1965/)
- Liu et al. 2024 suggest current models may be near the predictability limit of the CAMELS data, i.e. architecture has little headroom left given fixed inputs. — [Liu et al. 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10541025)
- He et al.: exogenous information reduced mean squared error by up to 40% on the largest dataset; models with comprehensive known exogenous inputs beat more limited approaches including foundation models. — [He et al. 2025a](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.15218v3); [He et al. 2025b](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.11849v2)
- Kratzert, Gauch, Klotz, Nearing (HESS Opinions, 2024): the amount and diversity of training basins is the decisive factor for LSTM skill; most published LSTM comparisons train on single basins and are therefore not informative about a properly trained model. — [Kratzert et al. 2024](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/4187/2024/)
- Where a non-LSTM clearly wins at scale (RiverMamba, +0.05 KGE at gauges), the model also adds spatial river-network context, so the gain is not attributable to the sequence cell alone. — [Shams Eddin et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22535v3)

### Inferences
- A defensible prior for this system: swapping the 2x128 LSTM for any published alternative with inputs fixed changes overall 6 h RMSE by somewhere between a small loss and a gain within seed noise, i.e. below the 0.1 cm threshold. This matches the in-house pattern already observed (third layer, longer look-back, per-type models, learned initial state all null): the model is not capacity- or memory-limited.
- The known weaknesses (2.5 h lag on rises; hurricane and urban-creek errors) are most plausibly information- and loss-limited rather than cell-limited: at 6 h lead on a fast creek, the rise is determined by rain that has not yet fallen, so skill is bounded by the rainfall forecast, and a squared-error-trained point forecast under uncertain timing hedges toward the last observation, which appears as lag. This is an inference; the supporting literature is thin (the pre-2022 abstract in which only input changes reduced phase shift, and the 2025 single-basin paper in which attention plus constraints only partly reduced lag).
- If architecture experiments are run anyway, the candidates ranked by fit to the task and cheapness, not by proven gain: (a) N-HiTS or TiDE with future covariates (cheap MLPs; one positive hourly result for N-HiTS); (b) TFT (tie at daily scale, native static / past / future input handling, slight peak-bias advantage); (c) xLSTM or Mamba as drop-in cells (no evidence either way); (d) Chronos-2 zero-shot and fine-tuned with covariates as an independent ensemble member rather than a replacement. Vanilla Transformer, Informer, Autoformer, PatchTST (no exogenous inputs in common implementations), iTransformer and TimesNet have negative or no supporting evidence for this setting.
- A structurally different model that is individually no better can still add value as an ensemble member alongside the five-seed LSTM mean, because its errors are less correlated than those of another seed. I found no hydrology paper quantifying this for mixed-architecture ensembles at hourly lead; it is a hypothesis.

### Gaps
- No published controlled experiment isolates architecture at hourly 1-6 h lead with real forecast covariates across hundreds of gauges; the "expected gain around zero" statement is extrapolated from daily benchmarks plus one hourly simulated-target study.
- No study found that quantifies architecture effects separately for extreme events (hurricane surge, flash floods) at hourly scale. The daily HESS benchmark states attention models do better on extreme flows, but the specific high-flow numbers were not in the text I could read.
- No quantified evidence on mixed-architecture ensembles vs same-architecture seed ensembles in hydrology.

## 6. Practical: open-source implementations and training cost relative to an LSTM

### Takeaway
Everything worth testing is available off the shelf with future-covariate support (NeuralForecast, Darts, NeuralHydrology), so the cost of a negative result is small for MLP models and xLSTM / Mamba, moderate for TFT, and several times to an order of magnitude higher for patch / cross-dimension Transformers. Published cost ratios vs an LSTM: about 3x (Non-stationary Transformer) to 13x (Crossformer) energy on the same hardware and epochs.

### Cited Findings
- Training cost on one A100, 30 epochs, CAMELS daily (531 basins): LSTM about 1 h and 0.17 kg CO2-equivalent; Non-stationary Transformer about 0.62 kg (about 3x); Crossformer about 2.25 kg (about 13x). PatchTST and TimesNet were restricted to one task because training time grew sharply with the number of basins. — [Liu et al. 2025, HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/6811/2025/)
- N-HiTS vs LSTM at hourly scale: "negligible difference in computational costs". — [EGU24-13353](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU24/EGU24-13353.html)
- Hourly LSTM cost reference (pre-2022): a naive hourly LSTM with long input took more than one day to train on a V100 for 516 basins; the multi-timescale version about 6-8 h. The Google daily encoder-decoder LSTM (5,680 gauges, 256 units) trains in about 10 h on one V100. — [Gauch et al. 2021](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/25/2045/2021/); [Nearing et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10954541/)
- RiverMamba (global grid, spatial): about 3 days on 16 A100 GPUs to pretrain plus 4 h to fine-tune; a different scale of system from a per-gauge model. — [Shams Eddin et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22535v3)
- Chronos-2: 120M parameters (28M small variant); about 300 series per second inference on one A10G at context 2,048 and horizon 64; zero-shot use needs no training. — [Chronos-2 report](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15821v1)
- NeuralHydrology (hydrology-specific, PyTorch): CudaLSTM, EA-LSTM, GRU, MTS-LSTM, MC-LSTM, ARLSTM, ODE-LSTM, Transformer (encoder only), Mamba (needs the mamba_ssm and causal-conv1d packages), xLSTM (needs the xlstm package), and the Handoff / Multihead / Sequential / Stacked forecast LSTMs. — [NeuralHydrology model docs](https://neuralhydrology.readthedocs.io/en/latest/usage/models.html)
- NeuralForecast (Nixtla) exogenous support by model: future + historic + static for LSTM, GRU, TCN, DilatedRNN, NBEATSx, NHITS, TiDE, TFT, TSMixerx, xLSTM, BiTCN, DeepAR, KAN; future only for iTransformer, TimesNet, Informer, Autoformer; historic + static only (no future) for TimeXer; none for PatchTST, TSMixer, NLinear, TimeLLM. All except the RNNs are direct multi-horizon models. — [NeuralForecast model overview](https://nixtlaverse.nixtla.io/neuralforecast/docs/capabilities/overview.html)
- Darts (Unit8) covariate support: past + future + static for BlockRNNModel, TFTModel, DLinearModel, NLinearModel, TiDEModel, TSMixerModel; past only for NBEATSModel, NHiTSModel, TCNModel, TransformerModel; future only for RNNModel; past + future for the wrapped foundation models Chronos2Model, TimesFM3Model, TiRex2Model. — [Darts covariates guide](https://unit8co.github.io/darts/userguide/covariates.html)

### Inferences
- Implementation choice matters for the two input types this system depends on. In NeuralForecast, TimeXer cannot take future covariates and PatchTST takes no exogenous inputs at all; in Darts, N-HiTS and TCN take past covariates only. A fair test of an alternative needs an implementation that accepts both past-only inputs (past rainfall, upstream discharge) and known-future inputs (forecast wind and rain) plus a station identity; in both libraries that narrows the list to TFT, TiDE, TSMixer(x), N-HiTS / NBEATSx (NeuralForecast), xLSTM, BiTCN and the linear models.
- Generic libraries train one model across series but do not provide the station-embedding, per-station-scaled change-from-last-observation target used here; those two design choices would need to be carried over, otherwise a comparison measures the target formulation rather than the architecture.
- For a model of this size (2 layers x 128, 72-step input), MLP models (N-HiTS, TiDE, TSMixer) and xLSTM / Mamba cells should fit within the same roughly 1 h job budget; TFT and patch / cross-dimension Transformers should be budgeted at about 3-13x based on the published ratios (those ratios are for 365-step daily inputs, so they are indicative only).

### Gaps
- GluonTS and PyTorch Forecasting: not checked in this pass (no source read); PyTorch Forecasting is the original home of a widely used TFT implementation, but I did not verify current model lists.
- No published timing for xLSTM or Mamba vs LSTM on hydrology tasks was found.
- No published fine-tuning cost for Chronos-2 on a dataset of this size (about 1,000 series x 13 years hourly) was found.
