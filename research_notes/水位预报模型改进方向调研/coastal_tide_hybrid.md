# Coastal / tidal gauges at 1-6 h leads: astronomical tide as a known-future input, hybrid physics + ML, hurricane inputs

Scope note: all numbers below are quoted from the cited source with its own baseline. "Macrotidal" / "microtidal" labels are added where transfer to the US Gulf coast (diurnal, range about 0.3-0.6 m) is uncertain. Items marked "not verified in this session" come from general background knowledge and were not confirmed against a source during this research; they are kept out of the Cited Findings lists.

## 1. Astronomical tide as a future covariate (residual target vs. tide as input; harmonic prediction pitfalls; CO-OPS API; constituents for non-NOAA gauges)

### Takeaway
Giving the network the harmonic tide for the forecast hours (or forecasting the non-tidal residual and adding the tide back) is standard practice in almost every published gauge-level ML study, including the long-running Texas-coast neural-network line, but no study was found that trains the same network both ways and reports the RMSE difference by lead time. On the microtidal Texas coast the tide table alone is a weak predictor (85% of errors within 15 cm at Rockport versus 95.8% for persistence), so the tide is useful as one covariate among several, not as the backbone of the forecast.

### Cited Findings
- Texas A&M University-Corpus Christi work (Texas bays, US Gulf, microtidal): neural networks with previous water levels, tidal forecasts, wind speed and direction, wind forecasts and barometric pressure "can greatly improve water level predictions" at open-coast and deep-embayment stations; wind forcing is described as the main variable missing from harmonic predictions, and tide charts perform particularly poorly in shallow Texas embayments — [Penn State Pure record of the TAMUCC paper](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/using-an-artificial-neural-network-to-improve-predictions-of-wate/); [AMS abstract](https://ams.confex.com/ams/SLS_WAF_NWP/webprogram/Paper46617.html)
- Rockport, Texas (shallow embayment), 1997-2001: central frequency of errors within 15 cm was 85% for the NOS tide table, 95.8% for persistence and 96.9% for the neural network; maximum duration of positive outliers 16 h (tide table), 14 h (persistence), 5.9 h (neural network); maximum duration of negative outliers 72.8 h, 0.6 h, 9.5 h. The lead time these figures refer to was not legible in the retrieved text — [Bowles, Tissot, Michaud, Sadovski 2005, Revista de Matemática: Teoría y Aplicaciones](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/453/45326946013.pdf)
- Texas-coast neural-network models with wind forecasts (NCEP Eta at that time) outperformed models without them and were "considerably more accurate than the tide tables" for leads of 1 to 48 h — [AMS abstract](https://ams.confex.com/ams/SLS_WAF_NWP/webprogram/Paper46617.html)
- Vicens-Miquel, Tissot and Medrano (Water, 2024), Gulf of Mexico tide gauges (open coast, embayments, ship channels; Port Isabel, Rockport and others): a sequence-to-sequence network gave errors within 15 cm for 92.2% of predictions at Port Isabel and 90.4% at Rockport at leads up to 108 h; the abstract states that existing models typically fail NOAA's 15 cm criterion beyond 48 h. The companion AMS abstract states the final models use only past wind and water level measurements and wind predictions — [NSF PAR record](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10567247); [AMS 2024 abstract](https://ams.confex.com/ams/104ANNUAL/webprogram/Paper436055.html)
- MET Norway residual-correction networks (Norwegian coast, not microtidal) take "tide estimates from harmonic analysis" as one of four input groups (with 24 h of observed water level, the numerical surge forecast and 10 m wind forecasts) — [Tedesco et al., arXiv 2301.00892](https://arxiv.org/html/2301.00892v4)
- Tampa Bay (St. Petersburg gauge, US Gulf, Florida): CNN-LSTM, LSTM and 3D-CNN received the harmonic tide fused in after the wind/pressure branch; during Hurricane Ian's negative surge (about -1.5 m) the plain LSTM "mostly followed the harmonic tide" (correlation 0.81 versus 0.94 for the CNN-LSTM). This is a direct illustration of a network leaning on the tide covariate when the meteorological signal is under-represented in training — [Ghahfarokhi et al., arXiv 2408.05797](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.05797v1)
- Chesapeake Bay (US Atlantic): a CNN-LSTM concatenates gridded numerical-weather-model wind features with astronomical tide levels in the LSTM units; mean RMSE 14.3 cm and correlation 0.94 in two extreme cases versus 12.30 cm and 0.96 for high-fidelity ADCIRC — [Shahabi and Tahvildari 2024, Coastal Engineering, via NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10567247)
- Southern North Sea preprint (macrotidal, 22 gauges): the tide is computed with UTide and added back to both observation and prediction, so RMSE on total level equals RMSE on the surge residual; reviewers asked that R² be reported on the residual, because R² on total level is dominated by the tide — [Wang et al. 2026, author reply](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-4129/egusphere-2026-4129-AC2-supplement.pdf)
- Residuals obtained with UTide often still contain leftover tidal signal, a known drawback of harmonic removal — [Tiggeloven et al. 2021, Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-96674-0.pdf)
- European surge networks (Hermans et al. 2025) define the target as hourly observations minus annual mean minus harmonic tide — [NHESS 25, 4593](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/25/4593/2025/nhess-25-4593-2025.html)
- Lunar diurnal K1 is the dominant constituent at many Gulf of Mexico stations (M2 on the Atlantic coast) — [arXiv 2505.18803](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.18803)
- In microtidal estuaries surge can dominate over the tide; classical harmonic analysis assumes stationarity and degrades where river discharge modulates the tide. NS_Tide puts the non-stationary forcing into the basis functions; a 2024 adaptation for the microtidal Neretva River (Croatia, Adriatic) added linear surge and linear + quadratic discharge terms and reported better predictions from the tide-dominated lower reach to the discharge-dominated upper reach — [arXiv 2411.13391](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.13391v1)
- Tidal river, Korea (Taehwa River): adding wind to an LSTM raised NSE of the tidal-level prediction from 0.67 to 0.90 — [search summary of the 2022 Water paper; see listing](https://mdpi.com/2073-4441/16/17/2452/xml) (secondary; the number was seen only in a search snippet, treat as unverified)
- NOAA CO-OPS Data API: `product=predictions` supports intervals h, hilo, 1, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30, 60 min. "Harmonic tide prediction stations can provide tide predictions on any available interval. Subordinate tide prediction stations can only provide tide predictions on a high / low interval." Request limits: 6-min data 1 month, hourly 1 year, predictions 1 year per request (high/low 10 years). Datums include MLLW, MSL, MHHW, NAVD, STND. Base URL `https://api.tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/api/prod/datagetter` — [CO-OPS API documentation](https://api.tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/api/prod/)
- For subordinate stations, predictions are made by applying time and height offsets to a harmonic reference station and only highs and lows are produced, so any value between them is an approximation — [NOAA Tide Predictions User Guide](https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/education/tech-assist/training/user-guides/assets/pdfs/Tide_Predictions_User_Guide_v4.pdf)
- The CO-OPS Metadata API has a station type `tidepredictions` listing stations with harmonic-analysis-based predictions — [CO-OPS MDAPI](https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/mdapi/latest/)
- STOFS-2D-Global publishes a model "harmonic tidal prediction" time series (`points.htp.nc`, 6-minute) separately from surge-only (`swl`) and combined (`cwl`) at 1,688 station locations, four cycles a day — [NOMADS STOFS description](https://nomads.ncep.noaa.gov/txt_descriptions/STOFS_Global_txt.html); [AWS bucket README](https://noaa-gestofs-pds.s3.amazonaws.com/README.html)
- NOAA examined in 1995 which parts of Laguna Madre, Texas count as tidal or non-tidal for datum purposes, i.e. NOAA itself treats parts of the Texas lagoons as non-tidal — [NOAA repository, Gill, Hubbard and Dingle 1995](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/2667)

### Inferences
- The calm-weather errors of 10-20 cm at estuarine and bay gauges "where the level turned within the 6 h window" are the error type a known-future tide covariate targets most directly: the turn of a diurnal tide is deterministic and currently invisible to a model that only sees the past 72 h of level (it must extrapolate the phase). Expected gain is largest in calm weather at open-coast and pass-connected gauges, and smallest at lakes, upper estuaries and the tidal river reach, where the tide is damped and lagged.
- Two designs are possible and the literature does not rank them: (i) tide as an extra future input channel with the total level as target, (ii) residual as target with tide added back. For a single global model over about 1,000 gauges of which most are non-tidal, design (i) is the lower-risk choice: the channel can be zero at non-tidal gauges and the target definition stays the same for all stations. Design (ii) injects any harmonic-prediction error directly into the forecast, which matters where harmonic predictions are poor (shallow bays, river-influenced gauges). The Tampa Bay Ian case shows the opposite risk for design (i): the network can over-trust the tide channel in rare surge events.
- For gauges without NOAA harmonic predictions (USGS, state and local gauges), the practical routes are: fit constituents to the gauge's own history with UTide (used in the North Sea study above); or use the tide at the nearest harmonic station with a learned lag/attenuation (the network can learn this if given the reference station's tide as input); or take the STOFS `htp` series where a STOFS station point exists. At diurnal-dominated sites a 1-year record resolves K1/O1/P1/Q1; river-influenced gauges need a discharge-dependent method (NS_Tide type) or should simply rely on the network with discharge + reference tide as inputs.
- Because harmonic predictions can be computed for any date, this input has no training-archive limit (unlike every hydrodynamic-model product below).

### Gaps
- No controlled ablation was found (same network, with and without future harmonic tide, RMSE by lead time 1-6 h). The only quantified "tide table vs. network" numbers found for the Gulf are the Rockport central-frequency figures above.
- No published accuracy statistics for NOAA harmonic predictions at Louisiana/Texas stations (RMSE of prediction vs. observation by station) were found; NOAA's list of stations where predictions are withheld or flagged as meteorologically dominated was not found.
- How many of the 51 NOAA gauges in the domain are harmonic (hourly predictions available) versus subordinate (high/low only) was not checked; this needs one call to the MDAPI `tidepredictions` listing.
- pytides and T_TIDE were not researched in this session (not verified in this session: both implement classical harmonic analysis; UTide is the commonly used Python/Matlab package that handles gappy records).
- The Narragansett Bay harmonic + LSTM-residual paper (Water, 2021, 3 h horizon) could not be opened (HTTP 403), so its numbers are missing.

## 2. Meteorological drivers for surge at short leads (pressure, wind over a fetch, 2D fields, buoys)

### Takeaway
Wind is consistently the most valuable added driver; pressure helps in hindcast-type surge models driven only by atmospheric fields, but added nothing once recent observed water levels and a numerical surge forecast were already in the inputs. Gridded wind/pressure patches (ConvLSTM, CNN-LSTM) give a modest gain over point inputs (about 5% RMSE in the best-documented comparison) and matter most in extreme or negative-surge events.

### Cited Findings
- MET Norway (Norwegian coast): with observed water level, the numerical surge forecast, harmonic tide and 10 m wind forecasts as inputs, "wind was the most important predictor"; wave and pressure data did not improve the networks — [Tedesco et al., arXiv 2301.00892](https://arxiv.org/html/2301.00892v4)
- Western Europe, 9 tide gauges, ERA5-driven (hindcast, 3-hourly, mostly macrotidal): inputs are u10, v10, wind speed and mean sea-level pressure in a 5° x 5° box (20 x 20 cells), 24 h look-back. ConvLSTM improved RMSE by about 5% and exceedance F1 by about 12% over LSTM. The hydrodynamic Global Tide and Surge Model had about 9% lower RMSE than the best LSTM; the best ConvLSTM came within 0.01 x P99 of it on average at six sites, but the hydrodynamic model was better by 0.08 x P99.9 for the highest extremes, and the networks "predominantly underestimate" events above the 99.9th percentile — [Hermans et al. 2025, NHESS](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/25/4593/2025/nhess-25-4593-2025.html)
- Same study: weighting the squared error by the inverse density of the target (DenseLoss, α from 0 to 5) reduced RMSE of values above the 99th percentile by 29% on average and raised exceedance F1 by about 9%, at the cost of accuracy for moderate values — [Hermans et al. 2025, NHESS](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/25/4593/2025/nhess-25-4593-2025.html)
- Global study, 736 tide stations, ERA5 predictors: LSTM generally outperformed ANN, CNN and ConvLSTM; skill much higher in mid-latitudes than in the tropics — [Tiggeloven et al. 2021, Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-96674-0.pdf)
- Baltic Sea (microtidal) random-forest study: the most relevant predictors are usually sea-level pressure, surface wind and "pre-filling" (the prior basin-wide level), and predictor importance depends on the gauge location — [NHESS 25, 1139 (2025)](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/25/1139/2025/nhess-25-1139-2025.html)
- Tampa Bay (US Gulf, Florida): inputs CFSv2 u, v wind and surface pressure on a 15 x 15 grid (0.25°, 26-29°N, 82-85°W) for 36 hourly steps plus harmonic tide; test R² 0.84 (CNN-LSTM), 0.77 (LSTM), 0.82 (3D-CNN); all three reach correlation 0.95 on the mostly calm test set, and the authors call the differences "marginal and relatively small"; the gridded model's advantage shows only in the hurricane case (correlation 0.94 vs 0.81) — [Ghahfarokhi et al., arXiv 2408.05797](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.05797v1)
- Texas coast: wind forecasts as inputs improved neural-network water-level forecasts at 1-48 h leads; barometric pressure was among the inputs of the early models but the 2024 models kept only past wind, water level and wind predictions — [AMS abstract](https://ams.confex.com/ams/SLS_WAF_NWP/webprogram/Paper46617.html); [AMS 2024 abstract](https://ams.confex.com/ams/104ANNUAL/webprogram/Paper436055.html)
- Tampa Bay area (NOAA analysis of currents): non-tidal residual flow correlates strongly with sub-tidal wind forcing at periods of 2 to 7 days — [NOAA repository](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/52289/noaa_52289_DS1.pdf)
- Chinese-language tropical-cyclone surge nowcasting study: the forecast improves more after wind is added than after pressure alone — [sciengine PDF](https://www.sciengine.com/doi/pdfView/F95633C105B445B0A46642E5997A1409) (seen in search snippet only)
- Adriatic (small tides, enclosed basin, seiche-prone; 11 gauges): the HIDRA3 network, with a shared atmospheric encoder and observations from multiple gauges, lowered mean absolute error by about 15% versus HIDRA2 and about 13% versus the Copernicus Mediterranean NEMO setup, and kept its skill when several gauges failed at once — [Rus et al. 2025, GMD 18, 605](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/18/605/2025/gmd-18-605-2025.pdf)
- Chesapeake Bay CNN-LSTM driven by gridded forecast winds + tide: 14.3 cm mean RMSE in two extreme cases versus 12.30 cm for ADCIRC — [Shahabi and Tahvildari 2024 via NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10567247)

### Inferences
- For the Gulf system, which already has past water level and local 10 m wind, the literature points to wind beyond the gauge (wind over the shelf / bay fetch for the previous 1-3 days and the next 6 h) as the next atmospheric input, ahead of pressure. On a shallow, wide shelf the setup responds to along- and cross-shelf wind stress integrated over a day or more (the 2-7 day band in the NOAA analysis), which a point wind at the gauge represents poorly.
- Pressure is cheap to add (same HRRR files as the wind) and physically relevant mainly in hurricanes (inverse barometer, roughly 1 cm per hPa, so a 30-50 hPa deficit is a 30-50 cm term). In ordinary weather its signal is small and largely already in the recent water level, which matches the MET Norway finding.
- A small 2D patch per coastal gauge (for example the HRRR u10/v10/pressure field coarsened to about 0.25° over a few degrees) is the published way to give "wind over the fetch"; the reported benefit is small in calm conditions and visible in extremes, so it should be judged on the hurricane subset, not the all-hours RMSE.
- Density-weighted loss (DenseLoss) is a directly transferable training change for the hurricane under-prediction problem, with a documented trade-off against calm-weather accuracy.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed US Gulf study was found that ablates pressure, offshore buoy observations or fetch-integrated wind for 1-6 h gauge forecasts with RMSE by lead time.
- Use of NDBC offshore buoys as ML inputs for coastal gauges: nothing found.
- No study found using HRRR or RTMA specifically as 2D inputs to a gauge-level water-level network.

## 3. Hybrid physics + ML: post-processing / residual learning on hydrodynamic forecasts

### Takeaway
Learning the numerical model's error from recent observations is the best-documented hybrid at short leads: gains over the numerical model are large at 1-6 h (36% at 1 h in Norway; about 66% at 2 h in the southern North Sea) but most of that short-lead gain comes from the observations themselves; the hydrodynamic input added only about 10% over a gauge-only network at 2 h. The value of the physics model grows with lead time and in storm events, where it supplies information the gauge history does not contain.

### Cited Findings
- MET Norway, Nordic4-SS (ROMS-based operational surge model): station-wise networks predict the residual of the already bias-adjusted forecast, one model per hourly lead from 1 to 60 h, using the target station plus four neighbouring stations. RMSE reduction at Oscarsborg (Oslo Fjord): about 36% at 1 h, 9% at 24 h, 5% at 60 h. At Bergen the networks gave almost no improvement in the first 24 h and increased bias. During storm Bella the 1 h error at Oscarsborg dropped by about 10 cm (about 25%). A simpler correction that bins mean error by wind speed and direction gave under 4%. Networks trained on hindcasts did not transfer to the operational forecast; the networks must be retrained when the numerical model is upgraded; training data were only about two years — [Tedesco et al., arXiv 2301.00892](https://arxiv.org/html/2301.00892v4)
- The operational Nordic4-SS product already applies a simple weighted-difference correction from recent observation-minus-model differences; the network was tested on top of that — [Tedesco et al., arXiv 2301.00892](https://arxiv.org/html/2301.00892v4)
- Southern North Sea, 22 gauges (preprint, not yet peer-reviewed; macrotidal): a hybrid LSTM + Transformer with gauge observations, COHERENS surge simulation and ERA5 forcing gives 2 h RMSE 0.055 m (station range 0.033-0.100) versus 0.061 m for the same kind of network on gauges only and 0.164 m for COHERENS alone; applied recursively to 12 h it reaches 0.146 m. Reviewers questioned whether forecast meteorology (rather than reanalysis) was used — [Wang et al. 2026, EGUsphere](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-4129/); [author reply](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-4129/egusphere-2026-4129-AC2-supplement.pdf)
- US coasts, ADCIRC hurricane forecasts (LSU-affiliated authors): an LSTM takes only the past offset series (ADCIRC minus observed, hourly; best look-back 15 h) and predicts the future offset, trained on 61 storms and tested on Hurricane Ian. Offset R² for Ian: 0.957 at 1 h, 0.922 at 3 h, 0.870 at 6 h, 0.827 at 9 h, 0.789 at 12 h, 0.737 at 18 h. Water-level RMSE before → after correction (units as reported by the paper, which works in feet): Clearwater Beach 0.917 → 0.069 at 1 h, 0.258 at 9 h, 0.519 at 18 h; Springmaid Pier 0.877 → 0.161 at 1 h, 0.570 at 9 h; Pee Dee River at Georgetown 0.911 → 0.239 at 1 h, 0.646 at 9 h, 0.858 at 18 h. Training on six varied storms was statistically indistinguishable from all storms at 12 h and beyond. Peaks improved little at longer leads; correction is only at gauges — [Giaremis et al. 2024, arXiv 2403.04818](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04818v1)
- StormNet (2026 preprint; 16 gauges on the US Gulf coast in FL, AL, LA, TX; test storm Idalia 2023): graph network (GCN + GAT + LSTM) on the same offset series, edges between gauges with correlation above 0.8 and distance under 500 km, best look-back 42-48 h. Average RMSE at 24 h 0.084 m versus 0.082 m for the LSTM of Giaremis et al. (no gain); at 36 h 0.084 versus 0.094 m (about 11% lower). RMSE reductions versus uncorrected ADCIRC at 48 h: Cedar Key 60%, Apalachicola 65%, Galveston Bay Entrance North Jetty 73%. The abstract's "more than 70% at 48 h" holds at one of the three stations shown; the paper is internally inconsistent on the number of training storms (11 or 13) — [StormNet, arXiv 2604.20688](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.20688)
- HURRI-GAN (2026 preprint) extends the offset correction to mesh points without gauges using generative adversarial networks and reports improved ADCIRC water levels at most test gauges — [arXiv 2603.06649](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.06649v1)
- Operational, non-ML analogue at NOAA: STOFS-2D-Global removes at each NOS/CO-OPS station the bias between observations and the most recent 5 days of nowcast; version 3.1 spreads that station correction over the whole grid by graph diffusion, so station and gridded outputs are both bias-corrected. STOFS-3D-Atlantic instead averages the nowcast bias of the past two days over 11 East Coast open-coast stations and applies it uniformly to the open boundary — [NWS Service Change Notice 26-51](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2026/scn26-51_STOFS.v3.1.pdf)
- Denmark: a stacked LSTM + temporal-convolution ensemble predicts the residual between tide-gauge observations and a storm-surge model, used to reconstruct and extend historical records (not real-time forecasting) — [ESSD preprint 2026-693](https://essd.copernicus.org/preprints/essd-2026-693/essd-2026-693.pdf)
- Immingham (UK east coast, macrotidal): an older neural network predicting the surge residual from distant gauges, wind, pressure and predicted tide achieved 6-24 h accuracy "comparable to or better than the UK national tide-surge model" — [CORE record](https://core.ac.uk/works/45840904)
- Adriatic: a pure deep-learning model (HIDRA3) beat the operational numerical model (NEMO, Copernicus Mediterranean) by about 13% in mean absolute error at gauges — [Rus et al. 2025, GMD](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/18/605/2025/gmd-18-605-2025.pdf)

### Inferences
- At 1-6 h, with a gauge observation available at forecast time, a hydrodynamic forecast is worth roughly a 10% RMSE reduction in ordinary conditions on top of a good gauge-only network (0.061 → 0.055 m at 2 h in the North Sea preprint). This is a macrotidal shelf-sea result; in the microtidal Gulf the tide part of that benefit is smaller but the wind-setup part (which the hydrodynamic model integrates over the whole shelf) is exactly what the present inputs lack.
- The hybrid is most valuable where the present system is weakest: hurricanes (estuaries 42.1 cm vs 8.3 cm at 6 h). In a hurricane the future level depends on forcing that has not yet reached the gauge; only a physics model (or track-aware inputs) carries that. The offset-learning studies show that the model error itself is highly predictable at 1-6 h (R² 0.96 → 0.87 for Ian), meaning "hydrodynamic forecast + learned offset from the last hours of observations" is a strong short-lead recipe during storms.
- Two ways to combine for the existing global LSTM: (a) feed the hydrodynamic forecast for the next 6 h as a known-future input alongside the wind and rain forecasts (the network learns when to trust it), or (b) make the target "observation minus hydrodynamic forecast" at gauges that have one. Option (a) keeps one model for all 1,000 gauges and degrades gracefully where the product is missing. Either way, the Norway result warns that hindcast-trained corrections do not transfer to operational forecasts: training must use archived operational forecast cycles, and retraining is needed after each model upgrade (STOFS changed in May 2024 and again in mid-2026).
- A network that sees neighbouring gauges (StormNet's graph, HIDRA3's joint latent state, Norway's four predictor stations) is a recurring ingredient; the gain appears beyond about 24 h in StormNet and is not demonstrated at 1-6 h.

### Gaps
- No published ML post-processing of STOFS, the NOS operational forecast systems, P-Surge or the National Water Model coastal output at gauges was found. A NOAA workshop presentation (Alipour et al., UIFCW 2025, [PDF](https://epic.noaa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/2.-Alipour-et-al-UIFCW25.pdf)) appeared in STOFS search results but its content could not be read, so what it covers is unknown.
- No documentation of operational ML post-processing at the UK Met Office / National Oceanography Centre, Deltares, Rijkswaterstaat, BSH, JMA, or Chinese or Korean agencies was found in this session. MET Norway's paper does not state that the network is deployed.
- No study compares hybrid, gauge-only ML and physics-only at 1-6 h on a microtidal, river-influenced coast.
- Verification statistics of STOFS at Louisiana/Texas gauges by lead time were not retrieved.

## 4. ML surrogates / emulators of storm surge (hurricane inputs)

### Takeaway
Surrogates are trained on libraries of synthetic-storm simulations and take storm parameters (track position, central pressure, radius of maximum wind, forward speed, heading) or wind/pressure fields as inputs; nearly all predict peak surge or a storm-long time series for planning or rapid ensemble use, not a gauge forecast updated hourly with observations. Their transferable ideas are the input set (track time series rather than landfall snapshot) and synthetic-storm training to compensate for the scarcity of real hurricanes.

### Cited Findings
- Lee et al. 2021 (Coastal Engineering 170, 104024; Chesapeake Bay): C1PKNet, a 1D convolutional network with principal component analysis and k-means clustering, predicts peak surge over a region from the time series of track conditions; trained on 1,031 high-fidelity simulations (landfalling and bypassing), checked against Isabel 2003, Irene 2011 and Sandy 2012. Motivation: earlier surrogates used the storm state at one moment near landfall, which is not always the state most correlated with peak surge — [Virginia Tech repository](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/handle/10919/108051)
- Kyprioti et al. 2021 used kriging with principal component analysis and k-means clustering for New Jersey / New York on synthetic storms; earlier Jia and Taflanidis work used kriging with PCA — [comparison table in Lee et al., Virginia Tech repository](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/bitstreams/5ed4b42d-cad2-4ca0-8e1b-7adc9174de30/download)
- Pachev et al. (arXiv 2204.13168): a multi-stage, point-wise surrogate for peak surge on the Texas and northern Alaska coasts, validated on Harvey 2017 and Ike 2008; predicting each point independently lets it give values at locations absent from training — [arXiv 2204.13168](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2204.13168)
- Kim et al. 2015 (Natural Hazards): time-dependent neural-network surrogate trained on 446 synthetic tropical storms for the Gulf of Mexico, six hurricane parameters as input, surge time series as output, validated at four points in southern Louisiana — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/nathaz/v76y2015i1p565-585.html)
- A gradient-boosting surrogate on a 446-storm Texas-landfall database reports a mean absolute error of 0.25 m for surge — [DeepAI listing](https://api.deepai.org/publication/learning-storm-surge-with-gradient-boosting)
- "Storm Surge in Color" (arXiv 2506.21743, 2025): encodes water-elevation fields as RGB images, convolutional LSTM with wind fields, 48 h forecasts for several Texas-coast regions, trained on synthetic Gulf storms — [arXiv 2506.21743](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21743v1)
- Encoder-decoder LSTM using storm position, central pressure and radius of maximum winds as encoder inputs and surge water level as decoder output, trained for leads of 3 to 12 h; several studies note accuracy falls quickly as lead time grows — [MDPI Atmosphere 14(7), 1082](https://mdpi.com/2073-4433/14/7/1082) (details seen in search summary only)
- Gharehtoragh and Johnson 2024: surrogate for peak surge in coastal Louisiana across future landscape scenarios, trained on ADCIRC; a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test rejected equality of the predicted and ADCIRC exceedance distributions — [AGRIS record](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/690c7d6ee36ca62843601246)
- 2026 preprint, north-western Gulf: point-wise feed-forward network producing spatially coherent surge time series from a simulation database; a referee noted that all training storms are perturbations of five primary synthetic tracks, limiting generalisation — [EGUsphere 2026-781](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-781/)
- New Orleans: active-learning emulator of maximum surge from ADCIRC runs (Thomas et al. 2023); a thesis chapter ("SurgeNet") starts a spatio-temporal graph network emulating ADCIRC on its own mesh and notes Fourier neural operators suit gridded rather than mesh data — [SurgeNet thesis chapter](https://sdat2.github.io/thesis/surgenet)
- MITONet (arXiv 2502.14782, 2025): autoregressive neural-operator emulator of coastal and riverine shallow-water dynamics (not driven by hurricane wind/pressure) — [arXiv 2502.14782](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.14782v2)
- Virginia Tech "LSTM Station Approximated Model" uses wind, pressure, rainfall and wave height to predict water level in hurricanes and transfers to stations outside training (news description; the paper could not be read) — [WVTF news item](https://www.wvtf.org/2025-06-17/machine-learning-can-it-help-predict-flooding-during-hurricane-events)

### Inferences
- For hourly gauge forecasting, storm-parameter inputs (distance and bearing from gauge to storm centre, central pressure deficit, maximum wind, radius of maximum wind, forward speed and heading, taken from NHC advisories for the forecast hours) are a low-dimensional way to tell the network "a hurricane is approaching and where", which the current gauge-local inputs cannot express. The surrogate literature shows these few parameters explain most of the surge response; no study was found that tests them as add-on features to an observation-driven LSTM.
- The training-data problem is the same one the surrogate community solves with synthetic storms: 2010-2022 holds only a handful of Louisiana/Texas landfalls per gauge. Options that follow from the literature: weight extremes in the loss (Hermans et al.), pre-train on simulated storms from an existing hydrodynamic model library, or let a physics forecast carry the hurricane signal (section 3).
- The finding in the offset-learning paper that six varied storms trained as well as 61 (at leads of 12 h and more) suggests storm diversity matters more than storm count.

### Gaps
- "DeepSurge"-type models, Jiang et al. and Bilskie et al. were not located in this session; no claims are made about them.
- No surrogate paper reports gauge RMSE at 1-6 h leads in forecast mode with real-time track errors.
- The quantified benefit of adding track/intensity parameters to a gauge LSTM that already has observations and wind forecasts is not documented anywhere found.

## 5. National Water Model, its total-water-level output, and river forecast center stage forecasts as inputs

### Takeaway
The products exist and are public (hourly National Water Model short-range forecasts to 18 h; coastal total-water-level runs with STOFS and P-Surge boundaries since version 3.0; river forecast center stage forecasts through the NWPS API), but no evidence was found that using any of them as a feature improves a short-lead ML gauge forecast.

### Cited Findings
- National Water Model short-range forecast: hourly cycles, 18 h, deterministic, forced by HRRR and RAP, initialised from the standard analysis, which runs hourly with a 3 h look-back and assimilates USGS streamflow — [NOAA Office of Water Prediction](https://water.noaa.gov/about/nwm)
- Coastal total water level (Atlantic, Gulf, Pacific): standard analysis with 3 h look-back; short-range forecast 18 h with ocean boundary conditions from STOFS; medium-range 10 days; additional short-range and medium-range runs with boundary conditions from both STOFS and P-Surge. Output in NetCDF, also SHEF for total water level, on NOMADS. Cycle frequency of the coastal runs is not stated on the page — [NOAA Office of Water Prediction](https://water.noaa.gov/about/nwm)
- The coastal coupling (freshwater routing linked to SCHISM) was introduced with version 3.0; output directories include `analysis_assim_coastal` for regions including `atlgulf` — [NWS Service Change Notice 23-76](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2023_24/scn23-76_national_water_model_v3.0_aab.pdf)
- The version 3.0 coupling is one-way (river to coast); a two-way approach is in development — [Hydrology 11(9), 145](https://mdpi.com/2306-5338/11/9/145)
- National Water Model retrospective version 3.0: February 1979 to January 2023, AORC forcing, on AWS at `s3://noaa-nwm-retrospective-3-0-pds/` (Zarr available since December 2023) — [AWS Open Data registry](https://registry.opendata.aws/nwm-archive/)
- River forecast center official stage/flow forecasts are served by the National Water Prediction Service API, e.g. `https://api.water.noaa.gov/nwps/v1/gauges/{id}/stageflow`; NWPS replaced AHPS on 27 March 2024 — [NOAA water API page](https://water.noaa.gov/about/api); [NWPS API flyer](https://www.weather.gov/media/owp/oh/docs/NWPS_API_Flyer_V6.pdf)
- An experimental API offers real-time and historical ensemble (HEFS) forecasts at NWPS locations — [NOAA water API page](https://water.noaa.gov/about/api)

### Inferences
- The retrospective run (1979-2023) is an analysis-type simulation, not archived forecasts, and nothing found indicates it contains the coastal total-water-level output (introduced operationally only with version 3.0 in 2023). So National Water Model total water level can be trained on only from late 2023 onward, which falls entirely outside the 2010-2022 training period.
- River forecast center stage forecasts are issued for a limited set of forecast points, typically at 6-hourly time steps once or a few times per day (not verified in this session); at 1-6 h leads at a gauge that reports hourly, their information content over the gauge's own recent trend is likely small except on large rivers during floods, where the present system already performs well.
- National Water Model streamflow for the next 18 h is a possible replacement for "upstream discharge" as a future input at estuary heads (compound events), and has a long retrospective for training, but with the caveat that training on a retrospective analysis and running on forecasts repeats the hindcast-to-forecast transfer problem reported by MET Norway.

### Gaps
- No study found that uses National Water Model output or river forecast center forecasts as features in a short-lead ML water-level model and reports the gain.
- Latency of National Water Model short-range and coastal files after cycle time, the cycle frequency of the coastal runs, and whether an operational-forecast archive exists on AWS (not verified in this session: a `noaa-nwm-pds` bucket with a rolling/partial operational archive) were not confirmed.
- River forecast center issuance frequency, forecast time step, the list of tidal forecast points for the Lower Mississippi and West Gulf centers, and archive depth of past issued forecasts were not found.
- Skill of National Water Model coastal total water level at Louisiana/Texas gauges was not found.

## 6. Compound-flood gauges (estuaries where rain-driven river flow meets surge)

### Takeaway
Published approaches for compound gauges are few and mostly unquantified: either a network that ingests both the rainfall/river side and the coastal side (CNN-LSTM with rainfall forecasts plus hydrodynamic output), or a surrogate trained on a hydrodynamic model run over synthetic compound scenarios. No short-lead benchmark shows which handles them best.

### Cited Findings
- Cork City, Ireland (EGU 2026 abstract; no validation metrics): CNN-LSTM for estuary and urban floodplain water levels at 1-33 h leads; the CNN extracts spatial patterns from rainfall forecasts and model outputs, the LSTM carries the water-level evolution — [EGU26-15017](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-15017.html)
- Pontianak, Indonesia (data-scarce delta): a hydrodynamic model generated compound-flood scenarios and ML models were trained on its output; the random-forest model predicted 11 of 17 compound events in the implementation phase — [Sampurno et al. 2022, Nonlinear Processes in Geophysics 29, 301](https://npg.copernicus.org/articles/29/301/2022/)
- Clear Lake, Texas (TU Delft thesis): LSTM surrogates trained on synthetic compound forcings reproduce the hydrodynamic response; an autoregressive variant feeds its own past water-level predictions back to infer pump response — [TU Delft repository](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:096bd18e-7278-4b8a-88fa-831d1023cb96)
- A CNN + LSTM with cluster-based temporal attention assimilating multimodal inputs was proposed to replicate compound flood dynamics (results not seen) — [NSF PAR record](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10640506/media/xml)
- NOAA's direction for compound flooding is physics-based: STOFS-3D uses National Water Model inflows (and GloFAS for the Pacific) "to include inland hydrology and extreme precipitation effects on coastal flooding"; version 3.1 refined the Atlantic mesh in watersheds, rivers, levees, barrier islands and the Intracoastal Waterway — [NWS Service Change Notice 26-51](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2026/scn26-51_STOFS.v3.1.pdf)
- In microtidal, river-influenced reaches, a harmonic model extended with surge and linear + quadratic discharge terms improved water-level prediction along the whole tidal river (Neretva) — [arXiv 2411.13391](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.13391v1)

### Inferences
- The estuary error in hurricanes (42.1 cm at 6 h) is the compound case; the inputs the literature combines there are exactly the ones missing from the present system on the coastal side (a downstream boundary signal: forecast surge or a downstream/open-coast gauge) while the river side (discharge, rain forecast) is already present. The most direct test is to give each estuary gauge the forecast or observed level of its seaward neighbour (open-coast NOAA gauge or STOFS point) in addition to upstream discharge.
- A 3D/2D hydrodynamic forecast that already includes river inflow (STOFS-3D-Atlantic, or a local 100 m coastal model) is the only input that represents the interaction itself; using its station output as a known-future feature is the hybrid route for these gauges.

### Gaps
- No quantified short-lead (1-6 h) ML benchmark at compound gauges on the US Gulf coast or elsewhere was found.
- No comparison of "downstream-gauge input" versus "hydrodynamic forecast input" for estuary gauges was found.

## 7. Real-time availability, latency and archive depth for the Louisiana / Texas coast

### Takeaway
Harmonic tide predictions and HRRR pressure/wind fields are available now with archives deep enough for training (tide: any date; HRRR: from late 2014). Hydrodynamic-model products that could be features (STOFS station output from 30 Dec 2020; NOS operational-forecast guidance from 2020; National Water Model coastal runs from 2023) are public and timely but have archives that do not reach back into the 2010-2022 training period.

### Cited Findings
- NOAA harmonic tide predictions: CO-OPS Data API, any interval at harmonic stations, high/low only at subordinate stations; up to 1 year per request for hourly; no archive limit stated (predictions are computed) — [CO-OPS API documentation](https://api.tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/api/prod/)
- CO-OPS observed meteorology at gauges: products `wind`, `air_pressure`, `air_temperature`, `water_temperature` from the same API (6-minute data limited to 1 month per request, hourly to 1 year) — [CO-OPS API documentation](https://api.tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/api/prod/)
- NOS operational forecast system guidance at gauges: product `ofs_water_level`, 6-minute model guidance at most real-time water-level stations inside a forecast-system domain, available from 2020 to present — [CO-OPS API documentation](https://api.tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/api/prod/)
- STOFS-2D-Global (ADCIRC): four cycles a day (00, 06, 12, 18 UTC); each run is a 6 h nowcast plus a 7.5-day forecast (180 h); station NetCDF at 6-minute resolution for 1,688 sites with combined (`cwl`), tide-only (`htp`) and surge-only (`swl`) series; SHEF at 30 minutes for 385 sites on MLLW; on NOMADS under `/pub/data/nccf/com/stofs/prod/` and on AWS in `noaa-gestofs-pds`, where daily folders run from 30 Dec 2020 (`estofs.YYYYMMDD` to 12 Jan 2023, `stofs_2d_glo.YYYYMMDD` after) — [AWS bucket README](https://noaa-gestofs-pds.s3.amazonaws.com/README.html); [NOMADS STOFS description](https://nomads.ncep.noaa.gov/txt_descriptions/STOFS_Global_txt.html)
- STOFS version 3.1: gridded + station bias correction for the 2D global model (based on the last 5 days of nowcast vs. NOS observations); new files without the correction (`points.cwl.noanomaly.nc`); the station points file is available about 15 minutes earlier than before; station NetCDF of the 3D Atlantic model now referenced to local mean sea level (previously NAVD88), SHEF stays on MLLW. STOFS-3D-Atlantic (SCHISM) file names in the notice show a 12 UTC cycle with a 24 h nowcast and forecast fields to 96 h. The notice gives the implementation as on or about 7 July 2026 at 12 UTC; an updated notice reportedly moved it to 17 August 2026 — [NWS Service Change Notice 26-51](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2026/scn26-51_STOFS.v3.1.pdf); [updated notice](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2026/SCN26-51_STOFS.v3.1_Updated.aaa.pdf)
- STOFS output "is NOT total water level guidance" in the official-forecast sense (NOAA disclaimer on the product portal) — [NOAA STOFS portal](https://polar.ncep.noaa.gov/estofs/glo.htm)
- P-Surge (SLOSH ensemble built from NHC advisories and historical track errors): version 3.2 implemented in mid-2026 (the MDL notices page lists 2 June 2026; another bulletin gives 26 May 2026); output on NOMADS as GRIB2 on a 625 m CONUS grid, file names carry cycle and storm ID — [P-Surge v3.2 notice](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2026/scn26-41_PSurge_v3_2_0.pdf); [MDL technical notices](https://vlab.noaa.gov/web/mdl/technical-notices-storm-surge)
- Extratropical surge guidance (ETSS and P-ETSS) was removed from the National Digital Guidance Database effective 2 September 2025; P-ETSS station output still exists (a station-output fix for the 06/18 UTC cycles is dated November 2025) — [MDL technical notices](https://vlab.noaa.gov/web/mdl/technical-notices-storm-surge)
- HRRR: archive on AWS (`noaa-hrrr-bdp-pds`) back to 2014; the hourly analysis series runs from 1 October 2014 to present and includes pressure reduced to mean sea level and 10 m wind components — [AWS registry](https://registry.opendata.aws/noaa-hrrr-pds); [dynamical.org HRRR analysis catalog](https://bcf14c32.dynamical-org.pages.dev/catalog/noaa-hrrr-analysis)
- RTMA is on AWS (`noaa-rtma-pds`) with a 10 m wind analysis; URMA is retrievable with the same tooling — [Herbie documentation](https://herbie.readthedocs.io/en/latest/gallery/noaa_models/rtma-urma.html)
- National Water Model: short-range 18 h hourly forecasts and coastal total-water-level runs on NOMADS; retrospective version 3.0 for February 1979 - January 2023 on AWS — [NOAA Office of Water Prediction](https://water.noaa.gov/about/nwm); [AWS registry](https://registry.opendata.aws/nwm-archive/)
- River forecast center stage/flow forecasts: NWPS API `api.water.noaa.gov/nwps/v1/gauges/{id}/stageflow` — [NOAA water API page](https://water.noaa.gov/about/api)

### Inferences
- Summary of what can be trained on 2010-2022 versus only recent years:
  - Harmonic tide (NOAA harmonic stations, or constituents fitted per gauge): full period, zero latency, known for all future hours.
  - Pressure and wind fields from HRRR: from October 2014 (the system already uses HRRR wind/rain, so the same files apply); before that a reanalysis would be needed.
  - STOFS-2D-Global station series (surge, tide, combined): archived operational cycles from 30 December 2020 only, so about two training years (2021-2022) plus the 2024-2025 test years; model versions changed in January 2023, May 2024 and mid-2026, which breaks stationarity of its errors.
  - NOS operational forecast system guidance at gauges: from 2020.
  - National Water Model coastal total water level: from 2023 (version 3.0), outside the training period.
  - P-Surge: only exists while NHC advisories are active (not verified in this session), gridded, not a continuous series; usable only as a hurricane-time feature.
- With four STOFS cycles a day and a 6 h nowcast, the newest available cycle at any forecast time is 0-6 h old plus production latency, so the 1-6 h target window usually falls at forecast hours of roughly 3-15 of the latest available run; the feature must be built from "latest cycle available at issue time", both in training and in operation.
- Datum handling matters for any hydrodynamic feature: STOFS station NetCDF is on local mean sea level (3D Atlantic changed from NAVD88 in version 3.1) and SHEF on MLLW; feeding the surge-only (`swl`) series or the anomaly relative to the model's own recent mean avoids datum mismatches at non-NOAA gauges.
- A locally run 100 m coastal hydrodynamic forecast for the same domains would play the same role as STOFS in a hybrid (known-future input or residual target) with higher resolution in estuaries, subject to the same requirement of an archive of past operational forecast cycles for training.

### Gaps
- Production latency (minutes after cycle time) of STOFS station files, NOS operational forecast guidance, HRRR and National Water Model files was not confirmed in this session; only the statement that the STOFS points file arrives about 15 minutes earlier in version 3.1 was found.
- Which Louisiana/Texas gauges are among the 1,688 STOFS station points (in particular non-NOAA gauges) was not checked.
- Whether a public multi-year archive of STOFS-3D-Atlantic station output exists, and its bucket name, was not confirmed (the 2D bucket README points to a separate registry entry).
- NDBC buoy real-time access and archive depth were not researched (not verified in this session: hourly standard meteorological data with multi-decade history are distributed by NDBC).
- RTMA/URMA archive start dates on AWS and whether URMA includes sea-level pressure were not confirmed.
- The date STOFS 3.1 actually went into operations is inconsistent between the original notice (7 July 2026) and the reported updated notice (17 August 2026); the updated notice itself was not read.
