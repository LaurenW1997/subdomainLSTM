# Spatial / multi-gauge / graph-based deep learning for 1-6 h water-level and streamflow forecasting

Notes compiled 15 Nov 2026. "Hourly" / "daily" is stated for every result. "Weak baseline" is flagged where the comparison model is not a global (multi-gauge, shared-weights) LSTM that sees the target gauge's own recent observations. Numbers are quoted from the page that was read (abstract page, HTML full text, or PDF text); where only an abstract was read this is said.

## 1. Graph neural networks on river networks: gains over a per-gauge global model, and cases where the graph did not help

### Takeaway
At hourly resolution and 6 h lead, the one study that isolates the effect of the river graph with the gauge's own recent discharge as input (358 Danube gauges) finds no gain from any graph definition over the same network with all edges removed. The positive GNN results are at daily resolution, without own-gauge observations as input, on large downstream basins, or against baselines that are not a global LSTM.

### Cited Findings

**Negative / null result at hourly resolution, 6 h lead**
- Kirschstein and Sun, "The Merit of River Network Topology for Neural Flood Forecasting": LamaH-CE hourly data, 358 gauges of the connected Danube network after filtering, inputs = past 24 h of discharge plus 4 meteorological variables at each gauge, target = discharge 6 h ahead, test years 2016-2017. Three GNNs (ResGCN, GCNII, ResGAT; 19 layers = longest path in the graph). Adjacency variants: no edges ("isolated"), binary, weighted by stream length / elevation difference / average slope, and learned edge weights; each in downstream, upstream and bidirectional orientation — [arXiv 2405.19836](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.19836v1)
- Mean NSE (percent, mean +/- std over 3 folds): ResGCN isolated 85.07 +/- 0.66 vs 80.68 to 84.13 with any edges; GCNII isolated 84.12 +/- 1.88 vs 83.90 to 85.56 with edges (best = bidirectional learned, 85.56 +/- 1.41); ResGAT isolated 83.10 +/- 0.88 vs 80.21 to 83.78 with edges; a 2-layer MLP reaches 85.37 +/- 1.64. All differences are inside the fold-to-fold spread; the authors conclude topology has negligible impact — [arXiv 2405.19836](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.19836v1)
- With no edges each GNN layer acts node-wise with shared parameters, i.e. the "isolated" model is a global per-gauge model, so this is a fair baseline (not a single-station model) — [arXiv 2405.19836](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.19836v1)
- Learned edge weights showed no consistent correlation with stream length, elevation difference or slope (Pearson r between -0.375 and +0.139, sign flipping between architectures) — [arXiv 2405.19836](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.19836v1)
- Lead-time table for the best model (24 h input window): NSE 99.05% at 1 h, 96.89% at 2 h, 94.20% at 3 h, 85.59% at 6 h, 75.98% at 9 h, 67.26% at 12 h; a 72 h window is similar or slightly better at short leads — [arXiv 2405.19836](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.19836v1)
- The worst gauge for every model has sudden narrow discharge spikes (the authors suspect a floodgate); they state that predicting such spikes matters more than adding topology — [arXiv 2405.19836](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.19836v1)
- Caveat in the same paper: on small sub-networks some edge configurations did help (sub-graph (ii): GCNII isolated 91.95 +/- 1.89 vs binary downstream-oriented 95.61 +/- 1.18), and the dataset has no gauge-to-gauge travel-time metadata, so lags must be learned implicitly — [arXiv 2405.19836](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.19836v1)
- No paper from the Kratzert group reporting "graph does not help" was found; the null result above is from a different group. The Kratzert-group items found are positive but without numbers: an EGU 2021 abstract (LSTM per sub-catchment + GNN routing, "promising" for daily and hourly simulation, no full paper found) — [EGU21-13375](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU21/EGU21-13375.html); and an EGU 2026 abstract on a global end-to-end semi-distributed model that is "superior ... especially for large, ungauged rivers" compared with the lumped model, no metrics or time step given — [EGU26-19528](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-19528.html)

**Positive results (check resolution and baseline)**
- Mosaffa et al. 2026 (HESS 30, 2079-2092), "A GNN routing module is all you need for LSTM Rainfall-Runoff models". DAILY, LamaH-CE Upper Danube, 530 gauged sub-basins, 180-day input, next-day discharge; inputs are ERA5-Land precipitation, soil moisture, temperature + 59 static attributes (no observed discharge as input). Mean NSE / KGE: baseline LSTM 0.46 / 0.49; LSTM-GCN 0.48 / 0.50; LSTM-ChebNet 0.59 / 0.58; LSTM-GraphSAGE 0.60 / 0.60; LSTM-GAT 0.61 / 0.65. Median RMSE 13.77 vs 21.24 m3/s (about 35% lower). Graph = directed downstream flow connections between gauged outlets, edge weight = inverse travel time from the Kirpich equation, self-loops — [HESS 30, 2079](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/2079/2026/)
- Where the gain sits in that study: LSTM-GAT better at 78% of stations; largest gains at downstream stations with many upstream nodes and large area; degradations cluster in high-elevation headwaters. Spearman correlation of the NSE gain with betweenness centrality 0.28, catchment size 0.25, number of upstream gauges 0.23; with elevation -0.20, slope -0.19. Three-station case: junction gauges 0.82 -> 0.92 and 0.76 -> 0.90, the upstream gauge no gain. Performance declined at 4 hops (mean NSE 0.51, attributed to over-smoothing). Peak timing "improved" is stated but no timing metric is reported. Random 70/15 train/validation split; single basin; a reviewer said the title overstates the scope — [HESS 30, 2079](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/2079/2026/); [preprint discussion](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2025/egusphere-2025-5008/)
- Sun, Jiang, Mudunuru, Chen 2021 (Water Resources Research 57): ChebNet, GCN and GraphWaveNet on CAMELS basins as nodes with learned relations; performance described as "similar or better than" an LSTM baseline (resolution not stated on the page read; CAMELS is normally daily) — [OSTI record](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1833425)
- HydroGAT (SIGSPATIAL 2025): HOURLY, two Iowa basins (Cedar 18 gauges, Des Moines 33 gauges), 4 km grid cells as nodes with D8 flow edges plus gauge-to-gauge catchment edges, 72 h in / 72 h out, Stage IV rainfall, May-September 2012-2018. Basin NSE: HydroGAT 0.97 / 0.97, DCRNN 0.95 / 0.97, GraphWaveNet 0.92 / 0.97, RGCN 0.90 / 0.97, GCRNN 0.86 / 0.84, STGCN-WAVE 0.76 / 0.90. WEAK-BASELINE FLAG: all five baselines are other graph models, no LSTM of any kind. Lead times evaluated start at 6 h (6-72 h in 6 h steps); no peak-timing metric; trained on 4 A100 GPUs per node — [arXiv 2509.02481](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02481v1)
- Sit, Demiray, Demir 2021: graph convolutional GRU on the upstream river network, HOURLY, next 36 h; reported better than persistence and a convolutional bidirectional GRU (abstract only, no numbers on the page; 4-page workshop paper) — [arXiv 2107.07039](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07039)
- Sun et al. 2026 (J. Hydrology: Regional Studies 64, 103275): GCN-LSTM with cross-station self-attention, DAILY and monthly, 97 GRDC gauges in the Danube; adjacency from great-circle distance plus similarity of static attributes (not the flow network); WEAK-BASELINE FLAG: baselines are MLP, RNN, GRU, LSTM, biLSTM. Station-thinning from 97 to 17 stations: skill stays high until fewer than about 30 training stations remain; accuracy at left-out stations drops with distance from training stations — [paper PDF](https://jadeys.github.io/assets/pdf/Sun_etal_2026_JoHRS.pdf)
- HydroNets (Moshe et al. 2020, Google): network-structured model combining each basin's rainfall-runoff signal with upstream dynamics, two large basins in India; the reported benefits are at longer horizons and with few years of training data, not at short leads — [arXiv 2007.00595](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.00595)
- RiverMamba (NeurIPS 2025): global 0.05 degree gridded discharge forecasts up to 7 days (DAILY), Mamba blocks along space-filling curves over river pixels, ECMWF HRES forcing; reported better than an LSTM-based model and GloFAS reforecasts, especially at longer leads and for extreme floods (abstract-level; no short-lead gauge results) — [arXiv 2505.22535](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22535)
- HydroGraphNet (2025): physics-informed GNN that emulates a HEC-RAS 2-D simulation on a 4,787-node mesh (White River, Indiana), 67% lower error than a baseline GNN (self-reported); it is a hydrodynamic-model emulator, not a gauge-network forecaster — [NVIDIA PhysicsNeMo docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/physicsnemo/25.11/physicsnemo/examples/weather/flood_modeling/hydrographnet/README.html); [author page](https://mehditaghizadehuva.github.io/portfolio/hydrographnet-physicsnemo/)
- Three ways of defining the graph in hydrology (survey): (i) physical flow network, (ii) distance / similarity, (iii) an a-priori matrix that is then adapted during training (the adaptive adjacency of Graph WaveNet / MTGNN) — [Survey, arXiv 2308.09766](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.09766)
- Semi-distributed LSTM + routing (Yu et al. 2024, HESS 28, 2107; Great Lakes, 141 training basins, 224 test gauges): on par with the lumped LSTM in training basins, more accurate on large basins (> 1000 km2) and in pseudo-ungauged basins — [HESS 28, 2107](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/2107/2024/)
- Background for the weak-baseline flag: Kratzert et al. 2024 (HESS 28, 4187) argue that LSTMs trained on a single basin are systematically worse than LSTMs trained on many basins, so "GNN beats single-station LSTM" does not show a benefit of the graph (this paper was not re-read in this session; cited from the published article) — [HESS 28, 4187](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/4187/2024/)

### Inferences
- The project's own tests (next gauge downstream: no gain; next gauge upstream: 3-4% lower RMSE at 127 stations, timing lag unchanged) agree with the only controlled hourly study: when a gauge's own last 24-72 h are in the input and the lead is 6 h or less, most of what neighbours could tell is already in the gauge's own series.
- Reported graph gains concentrate in three situations: no own-gauge observations in the input (rainfall-runoff simulation, ungauged reaches); gauges far downstream on large rivers where upstream-to-target travel time exceeds the lead time; and leads longer than about 6-12 h. Headwater and small, fast catchments show no gain or a loss (Mosaffa et al.), which is the category of the Houston urban creeks.
- A graph can only shift the timing of a forecast rise if some node upstream rises earlier than the target by at least the lead time. On a creek whose whole catchment responds within 1-3 h, no gauge fulfils that at 6 h lead; the information has to come from rainfall (observed and forecast), not from neighbours.
- Learned adjacency did not recover physically meaningful weights in the one study that checked, so "let the model learn the graph" is not a shortcut around defining travel times.

### Gaps
- No study was found that compares DCRNN / Graph WaveNet / STGCN / MTGNN against a tuned global LSTM with own-gauge autoregressive input at 1-6 h leads on hourly data. The HydroGAT comparison is among graph models only.
- No hourly GNN study on a mixed network of rivers, canals, tidal channels and lakes of the order of 1,000 gauges was found; the largest hourly gauge graphs found are 358 (Danube) and 51 (Iowa).
- No full paper for the Kratzert-group semi-distributed model (EGU 2026 abstract only), so its gain by basin size and its time step are unknown.
- Peak-timing metrics are almost never reported in GNN papers; claims of "better timing" are from hydrograph plots.

## 2. Attention across stations, set-based and transformer models that take several gauges jointly

### Takeaway
Evidence that cross-station attention beats a per-gauge global LSTM at hourly, 1-6 h leads is thin: the studies found are small (4-51 stations), compare against simple recurrent or convolutional baselines, and in one hourly upstream-inference test an LSTM beat a Transformer. The best-documented benefit of taking stations jointly is in the coastal setting (section 5).

### Cited Findings
- FloodGTN (Shi et al. 2023): HOURLY, coastal river system in Miami managed with gates and pumps and open to the tide (South Florida Water Management District data 2010-2020), water level at 4 stations, 72 h in / 24 h out. Covariates: rainfall, tide, gate and pump settings; a Transformer attends over covariates, a GCN-LSTM handles the stations, graph edges mirror the river layout. Average RMSE over the 24 h horizon with future covariates (ft): HEC-RAS 0.238, RNN 0.112, CNN 0.135, TCN 0.114, GCN 0.102, Transformer 0.091, FloodGTN-Series 0.094, FloodGTN-Parallel 0.079. Without future covariates: RNN 0.213, GCN 0.183, Transformer 0.175, FloodGTN-Parallel 0.169. WEAK-BASELINE FLAG: the recurrent baseline is a plain RNN; 4 stations; 80/20 split; "future covariates" include tide and structure settings assumed known — [arXiv 2310.07631](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.07631)
- In the same table the largest effect is not the graph but the known-future covariates: every model's RMSE roughly halves when future rainfall, tide and structure settings are supplied (e.g. FloodGTN-Parallel 0.169 -> 0.079 ft; RNN 0.213 -> 0.112 ft) — [arXiv 2310.07631](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.07631)
- Hourly upstream/downstream pair test (2026 preprint): 671 CAMELS basins linked to National Water Model reaches, target = NWM-simulated (not observed) hourly streamflow 1 h ahead. With local forcings only, normalised NSE 0.56 for both LSTM and Transformer; adding the forcings and discharge of the adjacent downstream reach raises it to 0.93 (LSTM) and 0.90 (Transformer); RMSE 2.24 -> 0.44 (LSTM), 2.41 -> 0.56 (Transformer). The LSTM is ahead of the Transformer in both settings. No handling of missing gauges; hyperparameters not tuned per model — [arXiv 2606.02791](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.02791)
- Sun et al. 2026 add multi-head self-attention across stations (each station a token) after a GCN-LSTM; DAILY/monthly only, baselines are single-sequence models — [paper PDF](https://jadeys.github.io/assets/pdf/Sun_etal_2026_JoHRS.pdf)
- Channel-dependence in generic multivariate forecasters: PatchTST is channel-independent, iTransformer treats each variable (here: each station) as a token, Crossformer attends over time and variables; an urban-water study reports that PatchTST, because of channel independence, gains little or nothing from added rainfall series — [Survey, arXiv 2411.05793](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.05793); [AquaCast, arXiv 2509.09458](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.09458)
- Hydra-LSTM (Ruparell et al. 2024): a mostly shared multi-catchment LSTM with a separate branch for inputs that exist only at some catchments (the abstract names upstream discharge as such an input); 1-day-ahead discharge, western US; reported more accurate than both multi-catchment and single-catchment LSTMs, and historical discharge could be added as a catchment-specific input without retraining the shared part (abstract only; DAILY lead) — [arXiv 2410.16343](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.16343)
- Google's operational stage forecasting (Nevo et al. 2022) is a set-style multi-gauge design: the LSTM receives the target gauge's past stage (168 h look-back) and a gauge-specific linear combination of the stages of "typically 2-5" upstream gauges reduced to five features (240 h look-back); HOURLY data — [HESS 26, 4013](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/26/4013/2022/)

### Inferences
- The South Florida result is the closest published analogue of canals controlled by gates and pumps near the coast: there, attention over structure settings and tide plus a small station graph improved on plain sequence models, but the dominant term was knowledge of future tide and structure operation. For controlled gauges, getting gate / pump / lock state (or a proxy) into the input is likely worth more than any cross-station architecture.
- A "set of neighbours" input (pool the embeddings of whatever upstream / nearby gauges exist, as in Hydra-LSTM or the Nevo linear combiner) is a smaller step from the present per-gauge model than a transformer over all stations, and it copes with stations that have 0, 1 or 5 usable neighbours.
- Transformers over station tokens scale quadratically with the number of stations; with about 1,000 gauges that pushes toward regional sub-networks or sparse (neighbour-only) attention.

### Gaps
- No hourly study was found that applies iTransformer / Crossformer-type channel-dependent models to a river or tide gauge network of hundreds of stations and compares against a global LSTM.
- No quantitative ablation of "with vs without upstream gauges" was found for the Nevo et al. system or for Hydra-LSTM at hourly leads.
- A reviewer of the 2026 Baden-Wuerttemberg operational LSTM preprint asked whether the model benefits from upstream stations; the manuscript trains in a lumped set-up without them, so this remains untested there — [egusphere-2026-4885 discussion](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-4885/)

## 3. Differentiable routing and physics-guided network models: effect on flood-wave timing at short leads

### Takeaway
Differentiable Muskingum-Cunge routing improves hourly simulation at large basins and narrows peak-timing error in a continental simulation (from 7-8 h to 4.5-6 h), but these are open-loop simulations without gauge assimilation; no evidence was found that routing reduces the timing lag of a 1-6 h forecast that already uses the gauge's own latest observations.

### Cited Findings
- Yang, Lonzarich, Song, Ji, Pan, Lawson, Shen (arXiv, September 2026), hourly US-wide differentiable HBV with multi-timescale structure and Muskingum-Cunge routing on more than 800,000 reaches: median hourly NSE 0.683 at 2,831 gauges vs 0.461 for National Water Model v3.0; relative peak-magnitude error for floods of 100-year return period or more 34% lower than recent AI models; captures 33% more floods of 50-year return period or more than NWM v3.0 and 159% more than an operational LSTM baseline; peak timing error narrowed from 7-8 h to 4.5-6 h. This is a simulation (no assimilation of gauge observations is described). The abstract does not say unambiguously which model the 7-8 h refers to — [arXiv 2609.06794](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.06794)
- Bindas et al. 2024 (Water Resources Research 60): differentiable Muskingum-Cunge on the Juniata network; a neural network infers Manning's n (and channel geometry) from reach attributes, trained only on the downstream hydrograph. More accurate routing at the training gauge and at untrained interior gauges than a spatially uniform parameterisation or simple summation of sub-basin runoff; benefit stated for sub-basins larger than 2,000 km2; channel geometry was not identifiable in synthetic tests and the absolute n values depend on the training period — [DOE EESM summary](https://eesm.science.energy.gov/publications/improving-river-routing-using-differentiable-muskingum-cunge-model-and-physics); [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/improving-river-routing-using-a-differentiable-muskingum-cunge-mo/)
- Short-lead test of differentiable routing (2026 preprint, three basins in Hebei, China: 2,210, 1,760 and 57.4 km2; leads 0.5-2 h; radar-echo inputs): differentiable Muskingum, convolutional unit hydrograph and an architecture-searched routing scheme were compared with a pure LSTM. The LSTM "achieves high numerical accuracy in some high-peak flood events"; the searched routing scheme was the most stable hybrid. No peak-timing numbers are reported, and a referee pointed out that interpolating records to 30 min may create artificial timing information and that peak-timing error should be reported — [egusphere-2026-3781](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-3781/)
- Semi-distributed LSTM with a separate routing model (Great Lakes, 224 test gauges): equal to the lumped LSTM at training basins, better for basins above about 1,000 km2 — [HESS 28, 2107](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/2107/2024/)
- The US National Water Model remains process-based; version 3.1 was scheduled to go operational on or about 18 August 2026, described as an hourly cycling analysis and forecast system — [NWS service change notice 26-64](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2026/scn26-64_National_Water_Model_V3.1.pdf)

### Inferences
- The timing errors quoted for the best differentiable routing model (4.5-6 h in open-loop simulation) are larger than the roughly 2.5 h lag at 6 h lead of a forecast that assimilates the gauge's own observations. Routing models therefore address a different problem (where the water is when no gauge is read); they are not shown to sharpen a short-lead, observation-driven forecast.
- Muskingum-Cunge is a kinematic / diffusive-wave scheme for one-directional river flow. It has no representation of tide, backwater, wind set-up, or gates, locks and pumps, so it does not apply to the tidal, estuarine, lake and controlled gauges, and its documented benefit begins at basin sizes (> 1,000-2,000 km2) well above urban creeks.
- For large rivers with several gauges in series, a routing-style inductive bias (edge weight = inverse travel time, as in Mosaffa et al.) is the form in which the graph helped in the literature; this fits the 127 stations with an upstream gauge better than the rest of the network.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that reports peak-timing error for a differentiable-routing model run in forecast mode with assimilation of upstream gauge observations at 1-6 h leads.
- Full text of arXiv 2609.06794 could not be opened (HTML version returned 404), so timing results by basin size and for small / flashy basins are not available here.
- NextGen (the US next-generation water modelling framework) operational evaluations of LSTM or differentiable components were not found in this search.

## 4. Several upstream gauges and rainfall over the contributing catchment: evidence for reducing timing lag in flashy urban creeks

### Takeaway
Operational practice (Google's stage models, lumped LSTMs) uses catchment-aggregated forcing and several upstream gauges, and the Kratzert group states that lumping hides where rain falls relative to the gauge; but no study was found that quantifies a reduction of peak-timing lag from catchment-average or travel-time-banded rainfall on small urban creeks at 1-6 h leads.

### Cited Findings
- Google operational stage forecasting, 2021 monsoon (HOURLY): linear and LSTM models both take past stage at the target and at typically 2-5 upstream gauges; LSTM look-back 168 h for the target and 240 h for upstream stages; operational lead times about 8-48 h on large rivers. Median persistence-referenced NSE 0.67 (LSTM) vs 0.60 (linear); LSTM better at 95% of gauges; adding precipitation improved the LSTM at 89% of gauges and the linear model at 65% — [HESS 26, 4013](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/26/4013/2022/)
- Kratzert et al. (EGU 2026 abstract): a lumped set-up "withholds information that is relevant to precisely predict floods", such as where a precipitation event falls relative to the prediction point, and spatial averaging over the upstream area weakens the precipitation signal; their semi-distributed model is reported better, especially for large ungauged rivers (no numbers) — [EGU26-19528](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-19528.html)
- Data integration (feeding recent observed discharge into a continental LSTM; DAILY): median NSE 0.86; largest benefit where flow is strongly autocorrelated; "even DI cannot elevate high-aridity basins with one-day flash peaks" — [Feng et al., arXiv 1912.08949](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.08949v2)
- Operational hourly LSTM in Baden-Wuerttemberg (138 catchments, 31-3,253 km2, median 165 km2) is trained lumped on catchment-averaged forcing and uses radar nowcasts for the first 1.5-2 h, then ICON-D2; it outperforms the distributed process model LARSIM at every lead to 21 h (numbers in section 7) — [egusphere-2026-4885 PDF](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-4885/egusphere-2026-4885.pdf)
- Short-lead radar study (leads 0.5-2 h): model attributions pointed to radar echoes and their extrapolation in the two basins of about 2,000 km2, and to measured areal and gauge rainfall in the 57 km2 basin (attributions questioned by referees) — [egusphere-2026-3781](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-3781/)
- Radar vs rain-gauge rainfall for urban stage forecasting (Sao Paulo dataset, preprint): ML models driven by 1 km radar estimates performed statistically comparably to those driven by 23 rain gauges — [ESSD preprint essd-2025-824](https://essd.copernicus.org/preprints/essd-2025-824/essd-2025-824.pdf)
- Flash-flood ConvLSTM study (Ellicott City, Maryland): water level peaks within 6 h of heavy rainfall; the authors argue a standard LSTM cannot extract spatial relationships among inputs (peak-timing results not seen in the excerpt) — [Frontiers in Water 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/water/articles/10.3389/frwa.2024.1346104/full)
- Full-grid vs basin-average vs single-cell rainfall input to an LSTM (snow-dominated basins, not urban): the authors stress "the critical importance of spatial input richness" — [MDPI Data 10(8), 122](https://mdpi.com/2306-5729/10/8/122)
- A graph over grid cells (rainfall at 4 km cells routed along D8 flow directions to gauges) is how HydroGAT injects travel-time structure; evaluated only from 6 h lead upward and only against other graph models — [arXiv 2509.02481](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02481v1)

### Inferences
- A 15 km box centred on the gauge is not the contributing area: for a creek gauge at a catchment outlet the box includes rain that never reaches the gauge and can miss the headwaters. Replacing it with rainfall averaged over the delineated catchment (and, for larger catchments, two or three bands by travel time to the gauge) is standard in every lumped or semi-distributed system cited above. The size of the gain for 1-6 h timing on urban creeks is not documented and has to be measured.
- For creeks with response times of 1-3 h, the 6 h forecast rise is driven by rain that has not yet fallen; the lag is then bounded by the skill of the rainfall forecast (HRRR / radar nowcast), and neither upstream gauges nor graph structure can remove it. Catchment-aggregated forecast rainfall and nowcasts for the first 1-2 h (as in the Baden-Wuerttemberg system) are the levers that the cited operational systems use.
- Several upstream gauges help where travel time between gauges is comparable to or longer than the lead time (the Google systems forecast at 8-48 h on large rivers with 240 h upstream look-back). The already observed 3-4% gain from one upstream gauge is in line with that; more upstream gauges should matter mostly on the long rivers, little on creeks.

### Gaps
- No controlled comparison "box at the gauge vs catchment-average vs travel-time-banded rainfall" with a peak-timing metric at hourly resolution on urban creeks was found.
- No published deep-learning study on the Houston bayou gauges with multi-gauge or catchment-rainfall inputs and reported timing error was retrieved (only single-watershed LSTM papers on Brays Bayou surfaced, without the comparison).
- The contribution of the upstream-gauge inputs in the Google stage models was not isolated in the paper.

## 5. Tidal and estuarine gauges: neighbouring tide gauges, offshore stations, joint multi-station coastal models

### Takeaway
For coastal water level the joint multi-station design has measured benefits: one shared model over 11 Adriatic gauges with stations randomly switched off in training is 7% better than the same model trained per station and keeps forecasting when gauges fail; on the US Gulf coast a 16-station graph model matched an LSTM at 24 h and was 11-12% better at 36 h. Combining gauges with a hydrodynamic model forecast adds more than the graph itself.

### Cited Findings
- HIDRA3 (Rus et al. 2025, Geoscientific Model Development 18, 605): one network for 11 Adriatic tide gauges; inputs are the past 72 h of sea level at the stations, past and future 72 h of wind, pressure, SST and wave fields on a 9 x 12 grid, and astronomic tide; output is hourly sea level for 72 h with a standard deviation per value. Station features are fused by averaging over the stations that have data; stations without data are estimated from the joint state — [GMD 18, 605](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/18/605/2025/)
- HIDRA3 accuracy: mean absolute error 8.0% lower than its single-station predecessor and 8.7% lower than the NEMO ocean model overall; on high sea levels 15.4% and 13.2% lower. Training the same architecture separately per station raised MAE by 7.4% (6.9% on high sea levels), i.e. joint training is worth about 7% — [GMD 18, 605](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/18/605/2025/)
- HIDRA3 with failed gauges: single-gauge failure -> still better than the offset-corrected NEMO at all locations; three northern gauges withheld together -> slightly higher MAE than with full input but still clearly better than NEMO there; all gauges missing for 72 h -> MAE up 35% overall and 43% on high sea levels (preliminary). Limitation: one missing value in a station's 72 h history discards that station's whole history. One year of data at a new station gave results comparable to ten years — [GMD 18, 605](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/18/605/2025/)
- StormNet (Nader et al. 2026, preprint): GCN + GAT + LSTM that forecasts the offset between ADCIRC and observed water level at 16 NOAA / TCOON gauges on the US Gulf coast, hourly, trained on 11 hurricanes, validated on Ian (2022), tested on Idalia (2023). Edges only between stations with Pearson correlation above 0.8 and distance below 500 km, weight = correlation. Average RMSE against a sequential LSTM (Giaremis et al. 2024): 24 h window 0.084 m vs 0.082 m (equal); 36 h window 0.084 vs 0.094 m, and 0.089 vs 0.101 m in the 2 days around landfall (11-12% lower, better at 11-13 of 16 stations). Bias-corrected forecasts had 60-73% lower RMSE than raw ADCIRC at 48 h at three stations (only one of three above the 70% in the abstract). Shortest lead evaluated is 6 h; stations with substantial missing data were dropped; a single test storm — [arXiv 2604.20688](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.20688v2)
- StormNet ablation (24 h, average RMSE): GAT + LSTM 0.097 m, GCN + LSTM 0.131 m, GCN + GAT + LSTM 0.087 m, full model 0.084 m — [arXiv 2604.20688](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.20688v2)
- Southern North Sea nowcasting (Wang et al. 2026, preprint): 22 tide gauges, 10-minute data, 2 h ahead for all stations at once, parallel LSTM + Transformer. Station-average RMSE at 2 h: gauges + hydrodynamic model (COHERENS) + ERA5 0.055 m; gauges only 0.061 m; operational COHERENS 0.164 m. Applied recursively to 12 h: 0.146 m, still below COHERENS — [egusphere-2026-4129](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-4129/)
- Multi-site graph convolutional recurrent network, Pearl River Delta: 5 tide stations, 48 years, tide plus meteorological inputs; better than five baselines; 1 and 3 h forecasts more accurate than 12 h (numbers not on the page read) — [AMiner record](https://aminer.org/pub/6425f6cf90e50fcafd7666ac/a-multi-site-tide-level-prediction-model-based-on-graph-convolutional-recurrent)
- Multi-graph spatio-temporal GCN, Seto Inland Sea: fuses three graphs (geographic distance, tide-level correlation, wind-driven dependence) with GRUs; 5 gauges; better than five classical models at 1-24 h and during Typhoon Jebi 2018 (numbers not on the page read) — [TRID record](https://trid.trb.org/View/2743882)
- Pearl River Estuary storm-surge "multipoint" models use a convolutional layer over tide levels at neighbouring stations (results not seen) — [Int. J. Digital Earth 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/10.1080/17538947.2025.2536074)
- Limits in storms: ConvLSTM surge models at western European gauges approach the skill of the hydrodynamic Global Tide and Surge Model at most locations but "still predominantly underestimate the highest extreme storm surges" — [NHESS 25, 4593](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/25/4593/2025/)
- PACT (2026 preprint): graph transformer with cross-attention from gridded atmospheric forcing to tide-gauge nodes, US Northeast coast, peak-aware; beats a spatio-temporal GNN baseline in RMSE and MAE (abstract-level) — [arXiv 2605.09036](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.09036)
- Regional LSTM over 31 Florida tide gauges predicting surge at all gauges; adding static local features per gauge (shelf width, shelter factor) improved it — [NSF PAR record](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10529651)

### Inferences
- The coastal setting is where a joint model has the clearest physical reason to help: surge and wind set-up are coherent over tens to hundreds of kilometres and arrive at open-coast gauges before they reach the back of an estuary, so neighbours carry lead-time information that an upstream river gauge does not.
- The largest documented gains in hurricanes come from adding a hydrodynamic surge forecast as an input or as the quantity to be corrected (60-73% below raw ADCIRC; 0.164 -> 0.055 m in the North Sea), not from the graph over gauges (0-12% vs LSTM; 0.061 -> 0.055 m from adding the model). For the 28.7 cm hurricane error, an external surge forecast as a future covariate for tidal and estuarine gauges is the better-supported direction; a station graph is a second-order addition.
- HIDRA3's recipe (shared encoder, masked mean over available stations, station switch-off probability 0.5 in training) is directly compatible with the existing practice of removing the newest hours in 25% of samples, and it was verified under single and regional gauge failure.
- Correlation-thresholded graphs (StormNet) need complete records at all nodes for training and were built for 16 stations; averaging over available stations (HIDRA3) avoids a fixed graph.

### Gaps
- No ablation was found that adds only neighbouring tide-gauge observations to a per-gauge LSTM and reports the change at 1-6 h on estuarine gauges.
- No published multi-station ML water-level model for the Louisiana / Texas coast at 1-6 h was found; StormNet starts at 6 h and predicts ADCIRC offsets during named storms only.
- No evidence was found on offshore buoys or shelf stations as inputs for inland estuary gauges.
- Operational status of HIDRA3, StormNet and the North Sea model was not confirmed.

## 6. Robustness to missing neighbours in real time

### Takeaway
The approach with operational backing is to embed each input group separately, average the embeddings of the groups that are present (masked mean), and train with random step-level and whole-sequence masking; whole-sequence masking is required, otherwise the model collapses when the input disappears. For neighbouring gauges the same idea has been validated on tide gauges with a 0.5 switch-off probability.

### Cited Findings
- Gauch et al. 2025 (HESS 29, 6221), DAILY, 531 CAMELS basins, three forcing products: compares (i) input replacing (zero-fill plus missing flag), (ii) masked mean (one embedding network per product, average those available), (iii) attention over embeddings. With random outages of 0-60% of time steps NSE falls at similar rates for all three; when a whole product is missing, input replacing is worst and masked mean / attention are within a small effect size (Cohen's d < 0.1) of models trained specifically on the available products; masked mean "tends to perform best" by a small margin and attention "appears unnecessary" — [HESS 29, 6221](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/6221/2025/)
- Google's 2026 global model is described as a "masked mean embedding LSTM" that keeps running during weather-data outages by averaging the embeddings of the input sources that are available — [EGU26-6973](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-6973.html). A search-result snippet attributes to the authors' presentation the statement that the masked mean is used operationally in the models on Flood Hub (the slide itself was not opened) — [Google Research publication page](https://research.google/pubs/how-to-deal-w___-missing-input-data/)
- Acuna Espinoza et al. 2026 (preprint; HOURLY; 138 gauges in Baden-Wuerttemberg; leads to 21 h): the masked mean is reused to assimilate real-time discharge. Meteorological inputs and lagged discharge (t-1, t-2, t-3 h) go through separate embedding networks and are averaged with a NaN-aware mean; the forecast is rolled out autoregressively. Training masks discharge with a step-level probability (random dropouts) and a sequence-level probability (whole discharge sequence removed) — [egusphere-2026-4885 PDF](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-4885/egusphere-2026-4885.pdf)
- Masking-rate ablation in that preprint (validation; median over 138 basins, averaged over leads; persistence-referenced NSE with discharge / NSE with discharge fully removed): step = sequence probability 0.05 -> 0.78 / 0.79; 0.10 -> 0.78 / 0.78; 0.20 -> 0.76 / 0.80; 0.40 -> 0.68 / 0.78; 0.60 -> 0.61 / 0.79; step 0.05 with sequence 0.00 -> 0.78 / -0.05. A stand-alone model without discharge input scores NSE 0.81. Chosen setting: 0.05 / 0.05 — [egusphere-2026-4885 PDF](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-4885/egusphere-2026-4885.pdf)
- The same authors warn that because discharge is highly autocorrelated, models become over-dependent on it and less sensitive to meteorological forcing, which degrades longer leads and is an operational failure risk when gauges go offline — [egusphere-2026-4885 PDF](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-4885/egusphere-2026-4885.pdf)
- Referee comments on that preprint: masking rates may not match real dropout patterns, which are often non-random; the benefit of assimilation is not clear from the figures; no comparison with LARSIM under identical gap conditions — [egusphere-2026-4885 discussion](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-4885/)
- HIDRA3 (tide gauges): each station switched off with probability 0.5 in training; fusion averages over stations with data; tested under single, regional and total gauge failure (numbers in section 5) — [GMD 18, 605](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/18/605/2025/)
- Simple practice in published systems: Google's 2021 stage models fill short stage gaps by linear interpolation and remove implausible jumps; HydroGAT fills gauge gaps by linear interpolation at the most downstream station and by station-specific linear regression on the downstream gauge elsewhere, with no evaluation of the effect — [HESS 26, 4013](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/26/4013/2022/); [arXiv 2509.02481](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02481v1)
- Graph imputation: GRIN (Cini et al., ICLR 2022) reconstructs missing sensor values with a bidirectional recurrent GNN and reports MAE improvements often above 20% on benchmarks; later work notes error propagation in its autoregressive design. It is bidirectional in time (a reconstruction method, not a real-time forecaster) and was not tested on water-level networks in the sources found — [arXiv 2108.00298](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.00298); [follow-up, arXiv 2205.13479](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.13479.pdf)
- Station-thinning of a graph model (DAILY, Danube, 97 -> 17 stations) left skill largely intact down to about 30 stations — [paper PDF](https://jadeys.github.io/assets/pdf/Sun_etal_2026_JoHRS.pdf)

### Inferences
- A neighbour-gauge input can be added without a new failure mode if (a) neighbours enter through their own embedding and are pooled by a masked mean over those available, and (b) training removes all neighbours in a fraction of samples in addition to removing individual hours. The ablation above shows that step-level masking alone does not protect against total loss (NSE -0.05).
- High masking rates cost accuracy when the data are present (0.78 -> 0.61 as masking rises from 5% to 60%), while robustness to total loss is already obtained at 5%. For an auxiliary input such as neighbours, a moderate rate is enough; HIDRA3's 0.5 applies to the primary input at a small number of stations.
- Fixed-adjacency GNNs (DCRNN, STGCN, Graph WaveNet) assume every node reports at every step; none of the hydrology GNN papers found evaluates real-time node outages, so the pooling designs are the documented route.

### Gaps
- No hydrology GNN paper was found that trains with node dropout and reports forecast skill under realistic (bursty, storm-correlated) gauge outages.
- No evidence on whether outages concentrated in storms (when gauges fail and when forecasts matter most) are handled as well as random masking.
- Exact NSE values per scenario in Gauch et al. are only in figures.

## 7. Operational examples of multi-gauge ML models and what they report

### Takeaway
Operational or near-operational ML systems are mostly per-gauge global LSTMs with no graph: Google's global model (daily, no river network in its description), the Baden-Wuerttemberg hourly LSTM (lumped, beats the distributed LARSIM model). The one operational system documented to use several gauges jointly is Google's 2021 stage forecasting on large South Asian rivers (2-5 upstream gauges, 8-48 h leads).

### Cited Findings
- Google stage forecasting, 2021 monsoon: 376 target gauges, stage models at 167 (LSTM used at 165), hourly, upstream gauges as inputs, lead times about 8-48 h, lead time shortened when the 20-80% uncertainty range exceeds typically 50 cm; median persistence-referenced NSE 0.67 (LSTM) vs 0.60 (linear model); median NSE of the LSTM with precipitation 0.99 — [HESS 26, 4013](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/26/4013/2022/)
- Google's current global model (Flood Hub; open-sourced 3 June 2026): DAILY streamflow, separate embedding network per weather product feeding an LSTM, static attributes plus forecasts (GraphCast, ECMWF IFS, IMERG, CPC); the description mentions neither gauge observations as real-time input nor river-network routing. Trained on nearly 16,000 gauges; accuracy at 7-day lead comparable to the predecessor at 5 days; reported better than GloFAS and GeoGlows in gauged and ungauged basins; the Czech hydrometeorological institute found it comparable to locally calibrated conceptual models — [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/the-next-chapter-in-flood-resilience-open-sourcing-googles-hydrology-framework/); [EGU26-6973](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-6973.html)
- Baden-Wuerttemberg flood forecasting centre test (preprint, September 2026; HOURLY; 138 gauges; same ICON-D2 forcing for both models; evaluation to 21 h): lumped single LSTM with discharge assimilation vs LARSIM (distributed process model with its own assimilation, operational in several European countries). Median NSE averaged over leads 0.89 vs 0.85, interquartile range 0.12 vs 0.17; NSE of the LSTM at 21 h (0.79) equals LARSIM at 15 h; persistence-referenced NSE 0.53 vs 0.40 (interquartile range 0.14 vs 0.41). For flows above each gauge's 95th percentile only: NSE 0.64 vs 0.57 (interquartile range 0.31 vs 0.42) and persistence-referenced NSE 0.55 vs 0.48 (0.16 vs 0.37). LARSIM's calibration period overlaps the evaluation period by six months — [egusphere-2026-4885 PDF](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-4885/egusphere-2026-4885.pdf)
- US: the National Water Model stays process-based (v3.1 scheduled for August 2026); LSTMs appear in research roles, e.g. as bias correction of hourly NWM output at USGS gauges; no NOAA operational multi-gauge ML water-level model was found — [NWS notice 26-64](https://www.weather.gov/media/notification/pdf_2026/scn26-64_National_Water_Model_V3.1.pdf)
- Candidate for the next National Water Model: the hourly differentiable HBV + Muskingum-Cunge model (median hourly NSE 0.683 vs 0.461 for NWM v3.0 at 2,831 gauges) — [arXiv 2609.06794](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.06794)
- Coastal: HIDRA3 is trained and evaluated against the operational NEMO set-up for the Adriatic with a 50-member atmospheric ensemble; the North Sea hybrid is compared with the operational COHERENS forecast (0.055 vs 0.164 m at 2 h) — [GMD 18, 605](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/18/605/2025/); [egusphere-2026-4129](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-4129/)
- Bureau of Meteorology (Australia) research: encoder-decoder LSTM forecasting water level up to 12 h ahead from antecedent states and near-real-time radar nowcasts at 15-minute input resolution (conference paper; details not read) — [MODSIM 2025 paper](https://mssanz.org.au/modsim2025/files/C07.hou.pdf)

### Inferences
- Groups with the most operational experience (Google, the Kratzert / Gauch / Klotz circle, the Karlsruhe group) have stayed with a single global LSTM and invested in input handling (multi-frequency inputs, masked mean, assimilation of the gauge's own discharge) before any graph; a semi-distributed successor is only at abstract stage in 2026. That is indirect evidence that the expected gain from graphs at gauged sites is small relative to its cost.
- The hourly LSTM beating a distributed, routed process model at every lead to 21 h in catchments with a median area of 165 km2 shows that explicit river-network structure is not necessary for good short-lead skill at gauged sites of that size.

### Gaps
- No operational report was found from a national service running a GNN or cross-station transformer for river stage.
- No European national-service (other than the Baden-Wuerttemberg test) or UK Environment Agency multi-gauge ML evaluation was retrieved.
- Google's description of its current model does not say whether gauge observations are assimilated; the 2021 upstream-gauge stage models may have been superseded.

## 8. Which methods are realistic for about 1,000 heterogeneous coastal and inland gauges with irregular data availability

### Takeaway
The evidence supports small, type-specific additions to the per-gauge global LSTM (pooled neighbour inputs with masking; a joint model with station switch-off for tidal clusters; catchment-based rainfall for creeks) rather than one graph network over all gauges; expected gains are single-digit percent except where an external hydrodynamic surge forecast is added.

### Cited Findings
- Hourly, 6 h lead, own-gauge input present: no gain from the river graph at 358 gauges — [arXiv 2405.19836](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.19836v1)
- Graph routing gain is tied to downstream position, catchment size and number of upstream gauges, and is absent or negative in headwaters (DAILY) — [HESS 30, 2079](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/2079/2026/)
- Joint tide-gauge model: about 7% from joint training, robust to gauge failure with 0.5 switch-off in training — [GMD 18, 605](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/18/605/2025/)
- Gulf-coast gauge graph: equal to LSTM at 24 h, 11-12% better at 36 h; hydrodynamic-model offset correction 60-73% below raw ADCIRC at 48 h — [arXiv 2604.20688](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.20688v2)
- Masked mean with step-level plus sequence-level masking keeps full skill when the input is present and falls back to the no-input model when absent — [egusphere-2026-4885 PDF](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-4885/egusphere-2026-4885.pdf)
- Controlled coastal canals: known-future tide and gate / pump settings halve the error of every architecture; with future covariates the graph-plus-transformer model is 13% below the Transformer and 29% below the plain RNN in RMSE, and the GCN alone is 9% below the RNN (percentages computed from the paper's table; 4 stations) — [arXiv 2310.07631](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.07631)
- Differentiable routing benefits documented for basins above about 1,000-2,000 km2 and in simulation mode — [HESS 28, 2107](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/2107/2024/); [DOE EESM summary](https://eesm.science.energy.gov/publications/improving-river-routing-using-differentiable-muskingum-cunge-model-and-physics)

### Inferences
Ranking by expected benefit per unit of effort (judgement, not measured):
- Most realistic, rivers with gauges in series: extend the single upstream-gauge input to a pooled set of the nearest 2-5 upstream gauges, each with its lag history, embedded separately and averaged over those available, with all-neighbours-removed samples in training. Expect a few percent at the stations concerned (the 3-4% already measured is the reference), mainly on long rivers; little effect on timing lag at 6 h unless the upstream travel time is 6 h or more.
- Most realistic, tidal / estuarine gauges: a joint regional model or pooled-neighbour input in which open-coast gauges inform estuary and channel gauges, trained with station switch-off. Documented gain about 7% from joint training; larger storm gains require an external surge forecast as a future covariate.
- Realistic, urban creeks: rainfall over the delineated catchment instead of a box at the gauge, split into travel-time bands for the larger creeks, plus a radar nowcast for the first 1-2 h. Physical reasoning and operational practice support it; no published number for timing lag exists.
- Realistic, controlled gauges: gate / pump / lock state or a proxy as an input; the only relevant study shows this dominates architecture choices.
- Less realistic: a single GNN (DCRNN / Graph WaveNet / STGCN type) over all gauges. Requires a defined graph across unlike water bodies, complete node data at each step or an imputation layer, and several GPUs at the scale reported; the controlled hourly evidence shows no gain at 6 h.
- Not suited to this purpose: differentiable Muskingum-Cunge routing. It targets simulation on one-directional rivers of large basins and has no tidal, backwater or structure physics.
- Any published gain measured against a single-station LSTM, a plain RNN, or only other graph models should not be carried over to a system that already uses a global LSTM with a station embedding.

### Gaps
- No published system forecasts rivers, urban creeks, tide gauges, estuaries and lakes within one multi-gauge model; all multi-gauge evidence is from one water-body type at a time.
- The hurricane case (the largest error) has no multi-gauge evidence at 1-6 h; the coastal graph work begins at 6 h and is tested on one storm.
