# Training-methodology improvements for a global LSTM that forecasts hourly water level 1-6 h ahead (no change of architecture family, no new input data)

Reading conventions used throughout:
- **[daily-sim]** = daily rainfall-runoff simulation without observed-flow inputs (most CAMELS work). **[daily-AR]** = daily with lagged observed flow as input. **[hourly]** = sub-daily. **[general ML]** = non-hydrology time-series or deep-learning result.
- "Transfers?" notes say whether the finding plausibly carries over to a 1-6 h forecast that already uses the latest observed level as input.
- "The existing model" = the global LSTM described in the assignment (station embedding, 72 h look-back, direct head giving the change from the last observation at 6 leads, test RMSE 2.2 / 5.3 / 8.4 cm at 1 / 3 / 6 h, 8.42 cm at 6 h for the mean of five seeds).

## 1. Timing lag / "persistence-like" forecasts: causes and remedies

### Takeaway
The lag is a known, 25-year-old property of data-driven forecasters that are fed the latest observation and trained with a squared error: the autoregressive input dominates, and the error-minimising answer to "a rise will come at an uncertain time" is a late, smoothed rise. Remedies with evidence fall into three groups — losses with an explicit timing or derivative term (DILATE: timing index down 6-14 % at about +10 % MSE on non-hydrology data; hydrology evidence is thin), withholding the autoregressive input in part of the training samples (Nearing et al. 2022), and judging models with a peak-timing metric — but no source shows a loss change removing a lag when the inputs contain no advance signal.

### Cited Findings

Cause (background, 2000s):
- de Vos & Rientjes (2005, feed-forward ANN, Geer catchment, daily and hourly): hourly forecasts show timing errors "as a result of a dominating autoregressive component", introduced by using previously observed runoff as an input. Replacing that input with other state indicators (moving averages of discharge and rainfall, soil-moisture output of GR4J) gave good timing but a worse overall fit than with previous runoff. Second proposed remedy: train with several performance measures at once. — [HESS 9, 111-126](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/9/111/2005/)
- The lag was not new in 2005: the interactive discussion of that paper points out that Minns (1998) had already identified prediction lags, that Varoonchotikul (2003) fed the modelled phase error back through a recurrent network, and that Gaume & Gosset (2004) and others had reported it. — [HESSD discussion comment S28](https://hess.copernicus.org/preprints/2/S28/2005/hessd-2-S28-2005.pdf), [HESSD discussion comment S82](https://hess.copernicus.org/preprints/2/S82/2005/hessd-2-S82-2005.pdf)
- de Vos & Rientjes (2008, WRR) introduced the mean squared derivative error (MSDE) as a training objective that "penalizes models for timing errors and noisy signals", used alongside MSE in multi-objective training; multi-objective training was competitive with and more consistent than single-objective training. No lag reduction in hours is quoted in the abstract. — [Univ. Twente record of WRR 2008, doi 10.1029/2007WR006734](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/multiobjective-training-of-artificial-neural-networks-for-rainfal/)
- Abrahart, Heppenstall & See tested a post-hoc timing-error correction procedure on River Ouse neural-network forecasts, starting from the observation that "neural network models will often produce phase-shift errors or timing lags". — [Hydrological Sciences Journal paper, record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/timing-error-correction-procedure-applied-to-neural-network-rainfall-runoff-modelling/811997513912418305-97347)

Modern LSTM evidence:
- Nearing et al. (2022) **[daily-AR]**, 531 CAMELS basins: feeding lagged observed streamflow (autoregression, AR) raised median NSE at 1-day lead from 0.796 (simulation LSTM) to 0.879; variational data assimilation gave about +8 %. Both AR and DA reduced the mean absolute peak-timing error and missed fewer peaks than the simulation model, but (as extracted from the article text) the remaining peak-timing error had a consistent sign for both methods, which the authors attribute to lagged streamflow carrying delayed information about peaks. No timing error in days is given in the text. — [HESS 26, 5493-5513](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/26/5493/2022/hess-26-5493-2022.html)
- Feng et al. (2020) **[daily-AR]**: with lagged observations as input ("data integration") the continental median NSE reached 0.86; the paper notes that the task then becomes predicting the change in flow, that the benefit is largest where flow is highly autocorrelated, and that data integration "cannot elevate high-aridity basins with one-day flash peaks". — [arXiv 1912.08949](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.08949)
- Gauch et al. (2021) **[hourly, simulation, no observed-flow input]**, 516 US basins: defines the peak-timing error used by NeuralHydrology (largest prediction within a window around each observed peak; mean absolute offset). Median hourly peak-timing error about 3.5 h for the multi-timescale LSTM versus more than 6 h for the US National Water Model; median hourly NSE 0.748. So a timing error of a few hours is normal for a US-wide hourly LSTM even when it is driven by observed (not forecast) rainfall. — [HESS 25, 2045-2062](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/25/2045/2021/hess-25-2045-2021.html)
- Kratzert et al. (2024) **[daily-sim]**: in their single-basin vs many-basin comparison the text speaks of "only small improvements in the timing and capture of hydrograph peaks" (context of this sentence not verified beyond the extracted summary). — [HESS 28, 4187-4201](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/4187/2024/hess-28-4187-2024.html)
- Ryd & Nearing (2025) **[daily-sim]**: per-basin fine-tuning of a globally pretrained LSTM moved the median peak-timing error from 0.537 to 0.500 d and the mean from 0.647 to 0.609 d — a small change. — [arXiv 2504.12559](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.12559)

Shape / time-distortion losses **[general ML unless stated]**:
- DILATE (Le Guen & Thome, NeurIPS 2019): loss = alpha x shape term (soft-DTW) + (1 - alpha) x temporal distortion index (TDI) term, both differentiable; cost O(k^2) in the horizon length k. Results, Seq2Seq model, mean of 10 runs (MSE x100, DTW x100, TDI x10): Synthetic step data — trained with MSE: 1.10 / 24.6 / 17.2; trained with soft-DTW only: 2.31 / 22.7 / 20.0; trained with DILATE: 1.21 / 23.1 / 14.8. Traffic — MSE-trained 0.89 / 24.6 / 15.4; DILATE 1.00 / 23.0 / 14.4. ECG — MSE-trained 21.2 / 17.8 / 8.27; DILATE 30.3 / 16.1 / 6.59. So timing distortion drops 6-20 %, MSE rises about 10 % (43 % on ECG), and soft-DTW alone makes timing worse. alpha = 0.5 (0.8 for traffic), smoothing gamma = 0.01. — [arXiv 1909.09020](https://arxiv.org/html/1909.09020v4)
- TILDE-Q (Lee et al., arXiv 2022): loss with three terms invariant to amplitude shift, phase shift and uniform amplification; the authors claim it beats MSE and DILATE in both MSE and shape metrics on electricity, traffic, illness, exchange-rate and weather benchmarks. Preprint; the per-dataset margins were not read. — [arXiv 2210.15050](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.15050v1)
- Groundwater level, hydrology-adjacent: "Deep learning models for groundwater level prediction based on delay penalty" (Water Supply, Feb 2024) proposes a loss combining Euclidean error, time warping and a time-delay term, and reports that it "significantly depresses the time delay with similar prediction accuracy" compared with MAE and MSE. Full text not obtained (403), so no numbers. — [DOAJ record, doi 10.2166/ws.2024.009](https://doaj.org/article/97aa9f323a964f08b78b237e346bcdf3)
- An EGU 2025 abstract (Piracicaba River, Brazil, 10-minute data) reports testing loss functions that prioritise flood periods, including peak flow and peak timing, while down-weighting normal and low flow; no results in the abstract snippet. — [EGU25-12740](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU25/EGU25-12740.html)

Input-side and decoding choices:
- Flood forecasting with LSTM/GRU and "lag-time preprocessing" (shifting upstream inputs by travel time; Water, Nov 2023): R^2 at 12 h lead rose from 0.8125 to 0.9215 for the best model. This is an input alignment, not a loss change. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/1218b916e1a2464191acc8bea9e13a50)
- Multi-step strategy comparison for LSTM runoff prediction (several basins, 1-7 d leads): recursive prediction lost the most accuracy with lead; the direct strategy was generally the most accurate; multi-output degraded least. — ["Effects of Multi-Step-Ahead Prediction Strategies on LSTM-Based Runoff Prediction", record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/effects-of-multi-step-ahead-prediction-strategies-on-lstm-based-runoff-prediction/1165217217793490949-3057)
- Kao et al. (2020) **[hourly, 1-6 h]**, Shihmen reservoir inflow: an LSTM encoder-decoder lowered RMSE by 3 % at T+1 up to 38 % at T+6 relative to its benchmark; a later Journal of Hydrology study found an encoder-decoder with exogenous inputs avoided the exposure bias of recursive decoding. — [record of "Exploring a Long Short-Term Memory based Encoder-Decoder framework for multi-step-ahead flood forecasting"](https://ersearch2.cvtisr.sk/vufind/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c455t-af5ef2d82bd5a55a8f62b453a5f11ea97cf7aed9b70395a96569f8d24737e29b3?lng=en)
- Scheduled sampling (Bengio et al. 2015) is the standard remedy for exposure bias in recursive decoders; it does not apply to a direct multi-lead head (from prior knowledge; page not re-read in this session). — [arXiv 1506.03099](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.03099)
- In an operational-style hourly LSTM with real-time discharge (138 gauges in Baden-Württemberg, leads to 21 h, preprint), the autoregressive roll-out is trained by teacher forcing with added noise, according to a referee's description of the appendix. — [EGUsphere 2026-4885](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-4885/)
- EGU 2024 abstract **[hourly]**: N-HiTS forecast time-to-peak and peak rate of hourly flood hydrographs more accurately than an LSTM and the National Water Model (different architecture family — outside the present scope, listed for completeness). — [EGU24-13353](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU24/EGU24-13353.html)

### Inferences
- Why the existing model looks like "last value plus damped trend": with a squared-error loss the optimal output is the conditional mean of the future change. If the inputs do not say *when* the rise starts, the mean over possible onset times is a late, flattened rise. This is the same mechanism de Vos & Rientjes described, and it matches the observation that upstream gauges, longer look-back and summaries did not change the 2.5 h lag: none of them adds information about the next 6 h of forcing. A loss change redistributes error (sharper and earlier rises, more false starts); it does not add information.
- What is cheap to test and has at least indirect evidence, in order of expected value:
  1. **A timing-aware selection metric.** Add a peak/rise-timing error (the Gauch et al. 2021 definition, or the cross-correlation lag already measured) to model selection beside RMSE, so that training variants are ranked on the lag as well. Costs nothing and makes every later test interpretable.
  2. **Derivative term (MSDE-style).** For a head that already outputs the change at 6 leads, a squared error on lead-to-lead increments is a one-line addition and is exactly the de Vos & Rientjes 2008 objective. Evidence is old and without lag numbers.
  3. **DILATE on the 6-lead output.** k = 6 makes the O(k^2) cost negligible. Expect, by analogy with the published tables, a timing-distortion gain of the order of 10 % at the price of several per cent more RMSE; with only 6 points per sample the warping path is short, so the effect may be smaller than on 20-100-step benchmarks. Soft-DTW alone should not be used (it worsened timing in the original tables).
  4. **More aggressive removal of the newest water-level hours.** The existing model already removes the newest 1-3 h in 25 % of samples; Nearing et al. recommend 50 %. Besides gauge-outage robustness this forces more weight on rainfall and other exogenous channels, which is the modern equivalent of de Vos & Rientjes' "replace the autoregressive input". Their result warns that overall fit can get worse as timing gets better.
- A measured hourly peak-timing error of about 3.5 h for a US-wide LSTM driven by observed rainfall suggests that the 2.5-3 h lag of the existing model at 6 h lead is not unusual, and that the part of it caused by missing future forcing cannot be trained away.
- If the lag cannot be removed, a probabilistic head (section 3) at least expresses it: the upper quantiles rise earlier than the mean.

### Gaps
- No study was found that tests DILATE, soft-DTW or TILDE-Q on hourly river stage or streamflow with a multi-gauge LSTM; the only hydrology-adjacent shape-loss paper (groundwater) could not be read in full.
- No source quantifies how much of a forecast lag (in hours) is removed by any loss function in hydrology.
- No paper was found that explicitly tests "predict the difference from the last observation" against "predict the level" with respect to lag; Feng et al. only note that the task becomes change prediction once lagged observations are inputs.
- Attention over exogenous drivers as a lag remedy: only single-basin case studies with attention variants were found (e.g. a physics-guided attention LSTM for 1-6 h leads on the Luan River with NSE 0.988 at t+1 and 0.908 at t+6, [HESS 29, 5955](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/5955/2025/hess-29-5955-2025.pdf)); none isolates the effect on timing.

## 2. Losses and sampling for extremes

### Takeaway
In multi-site LSTMs the published evidence for peak-weighted losses and oversampling is mixed to negative (two HESS studies in 2025-2026 found that weighting or oversampling extremes made results worse; positive reports are single-basin or unquantified), whereas three things help extremes consistently: per-site normalisation of the loss, training on more and more varied sites, and reading the upper tail from a probabilistic head.

### Cited Findings
- Frame et al. (2022) **[daily-sim]**: the LSTM had the lowest average peak-flow error in every return-period bin (bins of 1-5, 5-25, 25-50, 50-100, 100+ years; n = 5969, 1260, 185, 91, 84 events) compared with a mass-conserving LSTM, the Sacramento model and the National Water Model, and stayed competitive when events above a return-period threshold were withheld from training; adding a mass-balance constraint reduced skill on out-of-sample extremes. Numerical values are in figures only; a corrigendum exists. — [HESS 26, 3377-3392](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/26/3377/2022/hess-26-3377-2022.html)
- Kratzert et al. (2024) **[daily-sim]**: an LSTM's output is bounded by the head weights (upper limit = bias + sum of absolute head weights, in normalised-target space). In the test period only 10 observations exceeded the theoretical maximum of the 531-basin model, against more than 6,000 for the single-basin models — training on many basins raises the ceiling for extremes. — [HESS 28, 4187-4201](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/4187/2024/hess-28-4187-2024.html)
- Kratzert et al. (2019) **[daily-sim]**: basin-normalised loss NSE* = squared error divided by (basin discharge std + 0.1)^2, so that large-variance basins do not dominate. Against plain MSE, with static inputs: mean NSE 0.66 -> 0.69, median unchanged at 0.73, basins with NSE <= 0 from 6 -> 2; without static inputs: mean 0.24 -> 0.39, failing basins 44 -> 28. The gain sits in the poorly modelled basins. — [HESS 23, 5089-5110 (ar5iv copy)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1907.08456)
- Saadi et al. (2026) **[daily, stream temperature, 21 test stations]** — negative result: 18 loss variants that put more weight on the top 10 % of values "deteriorated the performances" for the top 10 % and for the whole range; none beat MSE on standardised targets significantly. What helped extremes was regional multi-site training with static attributes: top-10 % median MAE 1.29 C (single-site) -> 0.74 C (regional + attributes). — [HESS 30, 3623](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/3623/2026/hess-30-3623-2026.html)
- Martel et al. (2025) **[daily-sim, 88 Quebec catchments]** — negative result: oversampling (top 1 % of flows copied 10 times into training) gave the worst peak-flow errors of seven LSTM configurations and was dropped from the combined model; multi-head attention gave no appreciable gain; adding 500 donor catchments improved both KGE and peak error; adding a process model's simulation as input helped most. — [HESS 29, 4951](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/4951/2025/hess-29-4951-2025.html)
- Mosaffa et al. (2026) **[LSTM + graph routing, 530 sub-basins]** — positive but unquantified: duplicating the top 2.5 % highest-discharge sequences four times (share 2.5 % -> about 10 %) "improved the model's ability to capture peak magnitude and timing"; no peak metrics given, and a reviewer noted peaks were "still not consistently captured". Loss: station-weighted MSE with weights from discharge variance, normalised to mean 1 and clipped to 0.2-5. — [EGUsphere 2025-5008 / HESS 30, 2079](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2025/egusphere-2025-5008/)
- Peak-error losses PET / PES (peak error tanh / swish; single basin, daily, Upper Huai River): overall validation NSE 0.917-0.924; with PES the flood-period NSE is reported as 0.873. A later paper citing this work gives the comparison as flood NSE 0.747 -> 0.873 and flood-peak qualification rate 77.8 % -> 92.3 % at unchanged overall NSE (0.917); that the 0.747 baseline is an MSE-trained LSTM is likely but was not confirmed. — [record of the original paper](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/811909175377395712); [citing paper, Water 18, 1832](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/water/water-18-01832/article_deploy/water-18-01832.pdf)
- Baste et al. (2026) **[158 Swiss catchments]**: a deterministic LSTM underestimates more than 90 % of the top 0.1 % of observed discharges; the upper predictive bounds of probabilistic LSTMs capture 67 % of them; none of the probabilistic heads beats the deterministic model in NSE of the mean prediction. — [EGUsphere 2026-469 / HESS 30, 6339-6358](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-469/)
- Klotz et al. (2022) **[daily-sim]**: the deterministic LSTM was better than asymmetric-Laplace mixture models on the high-flow and low-flow bias metrics (FHV, FLV), although the mixture models were better on some other point metrics. — [HESS 26, 1673-1693](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/26/1673/2022/hess-26-1673-2022.html)
- Extreme-value-motivated variants: an LSTM with a switching mechanism motivated by extreme value theory for Australian flood forecasting — [arXiv 2407.15882](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.15882v1); a tail-weighted loss with a GEV transform for extreme precipitation — [Hydrology 12(11), 284](https://www.mdpi.com/2306-5338/12/11/284). Neither was read for effect sizes.
- Ryd & Nearing (2025) **[daily-sim]**: per-basin fine-tuning changed median peak MAPE from 45.18 to 44.18 % and missed-peak fraction from 0.546 to 0.537 — small. — [arXiv 2504.12559](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.12559)

### Inferences
- The existing loss (MSE on the change, scaled by a power-0.5 mix of each station's 6 h-change std and level std) is already a member of the NSE*-family; the literature gives no reason to expect a further gain from another global re-normalisation, and the Kratzert 2019 table shows such normalisation mainly rescues the worst sites rather than moving the median.
- Peak-weighted losses and event oversampling should be treated as low-prior experiments: the two controlled multi-site tests found they hurt, and the positive single-basin reports lack either a multi-site setting or numbers. If tested, the weights should be mild and clipped (as in Mosaffa et al.), and normal-condition RMSE must be reported next to event RMSE, since DILATE-type and tail-type losses usually trade one for the other.
- The saturation argument of Kratzert et al. (2024) maps onto the scaled-change target: a hurricane rise expressed in units of a station's own scale is far outside that station's training range but inside the range of other stations. This is an argument for more gauges with observed surge and flash-flood events in training (section 4), not for a different loss.
- That 34 of 432 stations are forecast too high in hurricanes by all five seeds (mean +4 cm) is a systematic conditional bias, not noise; neither ensembling nor loss weighting addresses it. It belongs to post-processing or to more event-rich training data, and with two storms in the test period any correction fitted to it cannot be validated independently.
- Urban creeks (57 cm vs 12 cm in hurricanes) resemble the "one-day flash peak" basins for which Feng et al. report that lagged-observation input does not help: the limit is rainfall information within the lead time.

### Gaps
- No controlled multi-site study of quantile/expectile, focal-style or density-based ("imbalanced regression") losses for hourly stage or flow was found.
- No study on event-based curricula (calm first, events later, or the reverse) for hydrological LSTMs was found.
- Exact values of the Frame et al. (2022) return-period figures were not accessible as text.

## 3. Probabilistic forecasting heads and calibration

### Takeaway
A mixture-density head with asymmetric Laplacian components (CMAL, 3 components) is the best-supported choice in hydrology and is what Google's operational flood model uses; Monte-Carlo dropout is the weakest tested option; conformal methods add coverage guarantees and benefit from online updating. Probabilistic training gives calibrated intervals and a usable upper tail but, on the evidence found, does not improve the point forecast.

### Cited Findings
- Klotz et al. (2022) **[daily-sim, 531 basins]**: four LSTM uncertainty methods — Gaussian mixture (10 components), CMAL (3 asymmetric-Laplacian components), UMAL, and Monte-Carlo dropout (MCD). CMAL was closest to the 1:1 line of the probability plot ("best performance overall"); the Gaussian mixture was under-confident; UMAL and MCD were over-confident at high flows; MCD was ranked weakest. Point accuracy of the distribution mean: UMAL best; CMAL and UMAL beat the deterministic LSTM on some metrics, the deterministic LSTM was better on high- and low-flow bias. Table values were not accessible as text. — [HESS 26, 1673-1693](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/26/1673/2022/hess-26-1673-2022.html)
- Nearing et al. (2024, Nature) **[daily forecast, 5,680 gauges]**: the operational global model outputs the parameters of a CMAL distribution over streamflow (hindcast LSTM on one year of weather + forecast LSTM on 7 days of forecasts, static attributes to both). — [Google Research blog on the Nature paper](https://research.google/blog/using-ai-to-expand-global-access-to-reliable-flood-forecasts/); [Nature 627, 559-563](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07145-1.pdf)
- Auer et al. (2024) **[daily, 531 basins]**: conformal prediction on top of an LSTM (HopCPT, a retrieval-based conformal method for time series) versus CMAL. CMAL had the best coverage and Winkler score overall, HopCPT close behind with the narrowest intervals; updating the conformal memory with new observations mainly improved coverage, and an update once per year roughly halved the coverage loss relative to never updating; for peak flows, information from other basins improved coverage because peaks are scarce at any single site. — [HESS 28, 4099-4126](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/4099/2024/hess-28-4099-2024.html)
- Baste et al. (2026) **[158 Swiss catchments]**: CMAL and two distributional-regression heads were "generally well calibrated, although some miscalibration remains for the extremes"; the heads differ mainly in sharpness and tail weight; mean-prediction NSE not better than the deterministic LSTM; upper bounds capture 67 % of the top 0.1 % of flows that the deterministic model underestimates in more than 90 % of cases. — [EGUsphere 2026-469 / HESS 30, 6339-6358](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-469/)
- Adaptive conformal inference (Gibbs & Candès, NeurIPS 2021) **[general ML]**: an online update of the miscoverage level that "provably achieves the desired long-term coverage frequency" irrespective of the data-generating process. Zaffran et al. (2022) show it works for time series with general dependence, that it loses efficiency in the exchangeable case and gains in an AR setting when its step size is well chosen, and propose a parameter-free aggregated version (AgACI). — [arXiv 2106.00170](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.00170v3); [arXiv 2202.07282](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.07282v1.pdf)
- Quantile regression on past forecast errors, conditional on the forecast value, is the classic water-level post-processor (Weerts et al. 2011, hindcast water-level forecasts in England and Wales). — [HESS 15, 255-265](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/15/255/2011/hess-15-255-2011.pdf)
- A prediction-interval LSTM (PI3NN-LSTM) reports a coverage of 0.89 for a nominal 90 % interval on one watershed. — [project page](https://sites.google.com/view/dan-lu/research-highlights/pi3nn-lstm-for-robust-time-series-prediction)
- A quantile-LSTM hybrid ensemble for extreme flows (six Australian stations, daily) flags over-estimation of the upper quantiles and recommends non-crossing constraints and post-hoc calibration. — [arXiv 2510.05453](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.05453)
- Deep ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al. 2017) **[general ML]**: several independently initialised networks, each with a Gaussian likelihood head, combined as a mixture; reported as better calibrated than MC dropout (from prior knowledge; page not re-read in this session). — [arXiv 1612.01474](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)

### Inferences
- The min-max of five seeds measures only the sensitivity to initialisation, not the spread of outcomes given the inputs, which is why observations left it in a calm-weather rise. Any of the following gives a real predictive distribution without changing the architecture family: (a) replace the 6-value head by a head that outputs CMAL parameters per lead (3 components x 4 parameters x 6 leads) trained by negative log-likelihood; (b) keep the deterministic model and fit conformal intervals per lead and station group on 2023 (validation) residuals, updated online with adaptive conformal inference; (c) a few fixed quantiles with the pinball loss.
- Because the seed-to-seed spread is tiny (+-0.04 cm) relative to forecast error, option (b) on top of the five-model mean is the cheapest route to calibrated intervals, and Auer et al. indicate that continuous updating matters and that pooling residuals across similar stations is needed for event conditions.
- Expect no RMSE gain from probabilistic training (Baste et al.; Klotz et al. mixed). The benefit for the timing-lag and hurricane problems is indirect: the upper quantile rises earlier and higher than the mean, so warnings can be based on it.
- Calibration should be checked separately for calm hours and event hours; all hydrology sources report residual miscalibration in the extremes.

### Gaps
- No calibration study at 1-6 h leads for stage with a multi-gauge LSTM was found; all calibration results above are daily.
- No head-to-head of CMAL vs quantile regression vs deep ensembles vs conformal for hourly water level was found.
- Table values (sharpness, NSE of mean) of Klotz et al. (2022) were not available as text.

## 4. Scale of training data, pretraining on a national/global gauge set, and fine-tuning

### Takeaway
"More and more varied gauges" is the best-documented lever in the whole field, including for extremes, and no hydrologically motivated subset has beaten the full set; pretrain-then-fine-tune adds a further gain of about 7 % in median NSE (14 % in the mean) in the one clean daily study, concentrated in poorly modelled sites, but it makes roughly one site in five worse. No measured transfer-learning result for river stage at short leads was found.

### Cited Findings
- Kratzert et al. (2024) **[daily-sim]**: LSTMs trained on a single basin are worse than one trained on 531 basins (reverse of what holds for conceptual models); median NSE rises monotonically with the number of training basins up to all 531; no subset grouped by hydrological unit (18 groups) or by attribute cluster (6 clusters) beat the full model on average; literature survey: only 28 of 150 LSTM papers trained on more than one catchment. Fine-tuning is explicitly out of that paper's scope. — [HESS 28, 4187-4201](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/4187/2024/hess-28-4187-2024.html)
- Gauch, Mai & Lin (2021) **[daily-sim]**: with restricted data, "it is key to train models not just on long time periods, but also on multiple basins"; LSTMs overtake tree models only with enough data; the best input sequence length depends on the training-set size. — [arXiv 1911.07249](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.07249)
- Ryd & Nearing (2025, ICLR workshop) **[daily-sim]**: LSTM (256 hidden units, 260 K parameters) pretrained on 6,375 Caravan basins for 40 epochs, then fine-tuned separately for each of 159 basins (all weights except the static-attribute embedding, or head only; 1-40 epochs, learning rate 1e-5 to 1e-3 found by search on validation NSE). Single-basin training: mean / median NSE 0.358 / 0.541. Pretrained: 0.473 / 0.583. Fine-tuned: 0.541 / 0.625 (median +0.042 = +7 %; mean +0.068 = +14 %; KGE median 0.683 -> 0.709). Gains are largest where the pretrained model is worst. Fine-tuning made 22 % of (model, basin) pairs worse (14 % of basins averaged over the 8 seeds); the variability ratio alpha-NSE was better before fine-tuning (0.881 vs 0.842). — [arXiv 2504.12559](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.12559)
- Ma et al. (2021, WRR) **[daily-sim]**: LSTM pretrained on US basins then fine-tuned with a few years of local data in Chile, Britain and China beat locally trained models; the benefit grew with the size of the source data set and exceeded pretraining on an uncalibrated process model. NSE values not read. — [Penn State record, doi 10.1029/2020WR028600](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/transferring-hydrologic-data-across-continents-leveraging-data-ri/)
- Konold et al. (2026) **[daily, 451 basins, 1-day forecast]** — negative result for one fine-tuning recipe: transfer learning that fine-tuned only the input embedding reached a median NSE of 0.44, against 0.63 for a forecast LSTM trained directly and 0.71 with recent discharge as input; pretraining with discharge and removing it for fine-tuning was consistently worse. — [HESS 30, 5067-5096](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/5067/2026/)
- EGU 2026 abstract (Mekong): fine-tuning a global LSTM on regional plus local streamflow raised median KGE from 0.65 to 0.72, better than regional-only or local-only fine-tuning (abstract only; which of the two EGU26 listings carries it was not verified). — [EGU26-2512](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-2512.html)
- A Frontiers in Water (2023) study pretrains one convolutional-LSTM on all gauges and then derives a model per gauge by transfer learning, reporting gains for snowmelt-driven and regulated flows. — [Frontiers in Water 10.3389/frwa.2023.1271780](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/water/articles/10.3389/frwa.2023.1271780/xml)
- Water level at tide gauges: Daramola et al. (2025, WRR) transfer bidirectional-LSTM-with-attention models from source to target tide gauges on the US Atlantic coast for extreme water levels; at target stations KGE 0.78-0.92, NSE 0.90-0.97, RMSE 0.09-0.18 m. No no-transfer baseline was visible in the material read. — [Virginia Tech repository copy](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/items/f9f5032a-c848-4512-b722-6a7acdbbdd5a)
- More sites help extremes specifically: adding 500 donor catchments improved peak error in Martel et al. — [HESS 29, 4951](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/4951/2025/hess-29-4951-2025.html); regional training with attributes lowered top-10 % error from 1.29 to 0.74 C in Saadi et al. — [HESS 30, 3623](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/3623/2026/hess-30-3623-2026.html); conformal intervals for peaks need information from other basins — [HESS 28, 4099](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/4099/2024/hess-28-4099-2024.html)
- Hourly large-sample data now exist beyond the US Gulf coast: 516-basin hourly CAMELS benchmark (Gauch et al. 2021) — [HESS 25, 2045](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/25/2045/2021/hess-25-2045-2021.html); CAMELS-DE-1h, 1,611 German catchments, regional LSTM median NSE 0.82 vs 0.69 for HBV (preprint) — [ESSD preprint 2026-289](https://essd.copernicus.org/preprints/essd-2026-289/); WaterBench-Iowa, 125 watersheds, hourly — [ESSD 14, 5605](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/14/5605/2022/essd-14-5605-2022.html)

### Inferences
- The finding that per-type models did not help the existing model agrees with Kratzert et al. (2024): subsets lose to the full set. The consistent direction of the literature is the opposite one — enlarge and diversify. Pretraining on all US gauges with hourly stage (several thousand) and then continuing training on the roughly 1,000 Gulf-coast gauges is the training-side change with the strongest literature support; strictly it uses more *target* data of the same kind, not new input variables.
- Expected size: the daily evidence (0.541 -> 0.583 -> 0.625 median NSE for single-site -> global -> fine-tuned) is from a simulation setting where the model must infer catchment behaviour from scratch. In a 1-6 h forecast with the latest level as input most of the easy variance is already explained, so the overall RMSE gain is probably much smaller; the plausible benefit is in rare regimes (surge, flash floods) that occur at only a few Gulf stations but many times nationally.
- Fine-tuning needs a guard: since about one site in five gets worse, per-station or per-region fine-tuned weights should only replace the pretrained ones where 2023 validation RMSE improves by more than seed noise. Fine-tuning only the station embedding is not supported (Konold et al.).
- For stage (not discharge) the scaling matters more than for discharge, because stage has an arbitrary datum and station-specific range; the existing change-from-last-observation target with per-station scaling already removes the datum, which is what makes national pooling feasible.

### Gaps
- No published measurement of pretrain-then-fine-tune for river stage at hourly leads was found; all quantified transfer results are daily discharge.
- No study with CAMELSH specifically was found in this search; no curve of skill vs number of gauges exists for the hourly forecast-with-observations setting.
- Meta-learning and hypernetwork conditioning versus a plain station embedding: nothing with measured gains in hydrology was found.

## 5. Static catchment attributes versus a learned station embedding

### Takeaway
For stations with long records, a learned embedding and static attributes are interchangeable (identical mean/median NSE in the direct comparison, and no gain from using both); several studies conclude that LSTMs use attributes mainly as station identifiers. Attributes pay off where an embedding cannot be learned — new or short-record stations — and their quality matters more than the model details there.

### Cited Findings
- Kratzert et al. (2019) **[daily-sim, 531 basins]**: adding static attributes to a multi-basin LSTM raised mean NSE from 0.39 to 0.69 and the median from 0.59 to 0.73 (basin-normalised loss); basins with NSE <= 0 fell from 28 to 2. Without any site information a multi-basin LSTM is poor. — [HESS 23, 5089 (ar5iv copy)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1907.08456)
- Shalev et al. (2019) **[daily-sim, 528 basins]**: replacing the attributes by a learned 20-dimensional vector per site gives mean / median NSE 0.69 / 0.73 with 1 failing basin — the same as with attributes (0.69 / 0.73, 2 failing); using both gives 0.70 / 0.73. Limits stated: an embedding needs enough data per site (9 years of daily data here; how few suffice is "an open question") and cannot serve sites without observations. — [arXiv 1911.09427 (ar5iv copy)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1911.09427)
- Li et al. (2022, WRR): assigning each basin a vector of random numbers instead of physical descriptors achieves comparable performance in the gauged setting, robust across data-sparsity scenarios and model choices. — [NSF PAR record](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10373035)
- Heudorfer, Liesch & Broda (2024) **[groundwater]**: all variants of static features performed equally well; the model "does not show entity awareness, but uses static features merely as unique identifiers" (in-sample mean NSE > 0.8; out-of-sample < 0.7). — [HESS 28, 525](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/525/2024/)
- Heudorfer, Gupta & Loritz (2025, GRL) **[daily-sim, CAMELS-US]**: the advantage of attribute-aware models is driven mainly by the meteorological inputs, with negligible contribution of static features, particularly in spatial out-of-sample tests. — [EGU25-16414 abstract](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU25/EGU25-16414.html)
- A 2026 preprint on ungauged basins concludes that the quality of static features matters more than architectural improvements. — [EGUsphere 2026-1965](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-1965/)
- Saadi et al. (2026) **[stream temperature]**: regional training *without* static attributes was often worse than single-site models; attributes were needed for the regional gain (one station: top-10 % MAE 1.18-1.34 C -> 0.48 C with attributes). — [HESS 30, 3623](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/3623/2026/hess-30-3623-2026.html)
- Regional LSTM for storm surge at 31 Florida tide gauges: adding local features per gauge (shelf width, shelter factor) improved performance (snippet only, no numbers). — ["Regional Modeling of Storm Surges Using Localized Features and Transfer Learning", record](https://vufind-test.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c750-c308b9a734a888b1c14d99b0aafccbd5422eb2c5fcdf7ca9786afd5f62ecec7c3)
- Gauch et al. (2025): feeding static attributes into the per-product input embedding networks hurt performance. — [HESS 29, 6221-6235](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/6221/2025/)

### Inferences
- With 13 years of hourly data per station, the 16-dimensional station embedding of the existing model is in the regime where attributes add nothing (Shalev et al.: both together 0.70 vs 0.69). Adding drainage area, slope, imperviousness, tidal range or distance to coast for the long-record stations is therefore unlikely to lower RMSE.
- Where attributes can matter: (a) stations with short records, whose embedding is poorly determined; (b) pretraining on a national gauge set (section 4), where an attribute-conditioned model can share behaviour across stations and a new Gulf station can start from attributes instead of a random embedding; (c) the 34 stations with a common hurricane bias, if an attribute (e.g. exposure to surge) explains which stations they are.
- Technically, attributes are extra inputs; if "no new input data" is read strictly they fall outside the scope, but they are static metadata, not a new real-time feed.

### Gaps
- No direct test of embedding vs attributes for stations with short records (e.g. 1-2 years) was found; Shalev et al. leave it as an open question.
- No study of attributes vs embedding for hourly stage forecasting with observations as input was found; with the latest level as input the site-identity signal may matter even less.

## 6. Data assimilation, autoregression and missing recent observations

### Takeaway
Feeding lagged observations directly (autoregression) beats variational data assimilation in accuracy and cost, provided the model is trained with observations deliberately withheld and told by a flag which values are missing; the recommended withholding rate is about 50 %, and for several input products a masked mean of per-product embeddings is the current best practice (used in Google's operational model).

### Cited Findings
- Nearing et al. (2022) **[daily-AR, 531 basins]**: AR median NSE 0.879 vs 0.796 for the simulation model (+10 %); DA about +8 %; AR better than DA on all metrics and in most basins; inference for 10 years over 531 basins: DA about 2 h on a V100 GPU, AR about 30 min, simulation under 5 min. Missing lagged flow is replaced by the model's own simulated value and a binary flag marks observed vs substituted. Training with 0 / 25 / 50 / 75 / 100 % of lagged flow withheld (mean gap length 5 days): less withholding is better when few data are missing at inference, more is better when many are missing; 50 % is recommended as the compromise; with the flag, AR almost always beats both the simulation model and DA up to high missing fractions; without it AR can fall below the simulation model; with everything missing AR equals the simulation model. — [HESS 26, 5493-5513](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/26/5493/2022/hess-26-5493-2022.html)
- Feng et al. (2020) **[daily-AR]**: median NSE 0.86 with lagged observations; feeding them directly is as good as passing them through a convolutional unit; moving averages, the last few days or even the previous month's mean all help. — [arXiv 1912.08949](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.08949)
- Gauch et al. (2025) **[daily-sim, three forcing products]**: three ways to handle missing inputs — (1) input replacing: missing values set to zero plus a binary flag per group (learning the fill value gave no gain); (2) masked mean: one embedding network per product, average of the embeddings of whatever is available at each step; (3) attention over product embeddings. Training with each product missing with probability 0-0.6 per step, and whole-sequence outages with probability 0.1. Masked mean tends to be best by small margins (significantly better KGE than input replacing at all but one missing rate, one-sided Wilcoxon, alpha = 0.05); attention brought nothing beyond masked mean; against dedicated models trained for exactly the available inputs the losses were small (Cohen's d < 0.1). At 0 % missing all three match the model trained without missing data. Masked mean is used in Google's operational flood model. — [HESS 29, 6221-6235](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/6221/2025/)
- Acuña Espinoza et al. **[hourly]**: a single-cell LSTM handling several input frequencies was 5 times faster than training on hourly data only (HESS 2025); the follow-up preprint combines it with masked-mean missing-data handling and real-time discharge so that the model improves when observations exist and keeps running during gauge failure; tested on 138 gauges with leads to 21 h against the operational LARSIM model (higher accuracy claimed, no numbers on the page; a referee describes the discharge update as a shift of the forecast starting point, i.e. closer to post-processing). — [HESS 29, 1749-1758](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/1749/2025/); [EGUsphere 2026-4885](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-4885/)
- Konold et al. (2026) **[daily, 1-day forecast, 451 basins]**: adding recent observed discharge to the hindcast part raised median NSE from 0.63 to 0.71 (sequential forecast LSTM) and from 0.57 to 0.66 (encoder-decoder); feeding archived weather forecasts into the hindcast part gave no benefit and destabilised training (mean NSE 0.57 -> 0.19). — [HESS 30, 5067-5096](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/5067/2026/)

### Inferences
- The existing practice (newest 1-3 h of water level removed in 25 % of samples) is the Nearing et al. recipe in miniature. Open choices that the literature speaks to: (a) the rate — 50 % was their compromise, and the right value depends on the real outage rate of the 443 real-time gauges; (b) an explicit missing flag per input channel, which Nearing et al. found necessary and Gauch et al. include in every variant; (c) gap lengths drawn from the real outage-length distribution rather than only 1-3 h (a referee of the 2026 preprint makes the same point: dropouts are not random).
- A model trained at one withholding rate is slightly worse on complete inputs than one trained without withholding (Nearing et al.), while Gauch et al. find no loss at 0 % missing with masked mean. Whether the 25 % removal costs RMSE on complete inputs in the existing model is a direct, cheap check.
- Extending the same dropout to the other input channels (rainfall, wind, pressure, upstream gauges) follows Gauch et al. and makes operation robust to a late or missing forcing file.
- Variational DA on the LSTM states is not worth pursuing here: it was less accurate and 4 times slower than AR even at daily steps.

### Gaps
- All quantified results are daily; no published sweep of withholding rate vs skill exists for hourly stage at 1-6 h.
- No numbers for the accuracy of the hourly operational-style model (EGUsphere 2026-4885) with and without real-time discharge were available.

## 7. Ensembling beyond seeds

### Takeaway
Seed ensembles of 8-10 are standard in the leading groups and mainly remove bad outliers rather than move the median; no hydrology study of snapshot ensembles, stochastic weight averaging or exponential moving averages of weights was found, so these single-model substitutes rest on general deep-learning evidence only.

### Cited Findings
- Kratzert et al. (2019) **[daily-sim]**: mean of 8 seeds vs a single model — without static inputs mean NSE 0.39 -> 0.49 and failing basins 28 -> 20 (median 0.59 -> 0.64); attribute-aware LSTM, MSE loss: mean 0.63 -> 0.68, median 0.71 -> 0.74, failing 9 -> 6; with the basin-normalised loss 0.67 -> 0.70, median 0.71 -> 0.74. Seed-to-seed std of mean NSE 0.006-0.059. — [HESS 23, 5089 (ar5iv copy)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1907.08456)
- Kratzert et al. (2024): 10 seeds per configuration, hydrographs averaged, recommended as standard practice. — [HESS 28, 4187](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/4187/2024/hess-28-4187-2024.html); Gauch et al. (2021): 10 seeds — [HESS 25, 2045](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/25/2045/2021/hess-25-2045-2021.html); Ryd & Nearing (2025): 8 pretrained seeds — [arXiv 2504.12559](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.12559)
- Replication with the open Kratzert code: differences in median NSE between runs "well below 0.01", attributable to non-determinism. — [arXiv 1911.07249 (ar5iv copy)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1911.07249)
- Stochastic weight averaging (Izmailov et al. 2018) **[general ML, image classification]**: averaging weights along the SGD trajectory with a cyclical or constant learning rate "leads to better generalization than conventional training", finds flatter solutions, approximates Fast Geometric Ensembling "with a single model", with "almost no computational overhead"; no numbers in the abstract. — [arXiv 1803.05407](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.05407)
- Snapshot ensembles (Huang et al. 2017) **[general ML]**: cyclic learning rate, save the model at the end of each cycle, average the snapshots — an ensemble from one training run (from prior knowledge; page not re-read in this session). — [arXiv 1704.00109](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.00109)
- Stacking with a different information source helps where plain variants do not: feeding a process model's simulation into the LSTM had the largest single effect on peaks in Martel et al., whereas architectural variants (multi-head) did not. — [HESS 29, 4951](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/4951/2025/hess-29-4951-2025.html)

### Inferences
- The existing result (five seeds: 8.66 -> 8.42 cm, -2.8 %, with 8.51 / 8.47 / 8.44 / 8.42 for 2-5 members) is in line with the literature pattern of small median gains; the published 8-10 members would add perhaps another 0.02-0.03 cm by extrapolation of the diminishing series. The stronger argument for the ensemble is the one in Kratzert 2019: it removes single-seed failures, which matches the observed factor-of-two differences between seeds at single stations in hurricanes.
- Members that differ only in seed are highly correlated. Diversity that costs no new data: different look-back lengths, different withholding rates of recent observations, different losses (MSE member + DILATE-type member + quantile member), different training-year subsets. Variants that were individually "no gain" (longer look-back, third layer) can still be useful ensemble members if their errors are less correlated — this is testable from saved predictions without retraining.
- Weight averaging (SWA or an exponential moving average over the last epochs) and snapshot ensembling are the candidates for ensemble-like gains at single-model inference cost; with 40 epochs x 2000 steps the last 10 epochs could be averaged at no cost. No hydrology evidence exists, so the outcome is uncertain. Note that weight averaging across independently seeded models does not work (different basins of the loss surface); it must be along one trajectory.
- The systematic hurricane bias at 34 stations is common to all seeds, so no seed ensemble can remove it.

### Gaps
- No hydrology paper giving skill as a function of ensemble size (1, 2, 4, 8, 16) was found; nor any on SWA, EMA, snapshot ensembles, model soups or diversity-encouraging training for LSTM streamflow or stage models.
- No quantified comparison of stacking different LSTM input sets versus seed ensembles was found.

## 8. Post-processing: bias correction and online error updating

### Takeaway
Autoregressive error updating is most valuable in the first steps and decays fast — a classic result has its benefit gone after six time steps, and a 2025 comparison shows an ARIMA corrector dropping from NSE 0.97 to 0.29 over 24 h — while learned (LSTM) correctors hold up better at 2-6 h; almost all evidence is for correcting process models, not for correcting an LSTM that already ingests the latest observation.

### Cited Findings
- Gegenleithner et al. (2025) **[15-min data, one 78 km^2 Austrian catchment, 24 h horizon]**: ARIMA(5,1,6) on the residuals of a process model vs hindcast-forecast LSTM correctors. ARIMA (2017): KGE about 0.98 at the first step falling to 0.60 at the last, NSE 0.97 -> 0.29; the LSTM correctors had larger errors in the first steps but were more reliable on average after three steps (45 min) and kept their accuracy over the horizon. — [HESS 29, 1939](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/1939/2025/)
- Brath et al. (2002), as cited in that literature: the accuracy of an adaptively updated ARIMA-type corrector fell to that of the non-updated model after six time steps. — [cited in HESS 29, 1939](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/1939/2025/hess-29-1939-2025.html)
- Hu, Li & Wu **[hourly, 1-6 h leads, middle Yellow River]**: an ARMAX corrector was acceptable at 1 h (NSE > 0.7) but poor at 2-6 h; an LSTM corrector was best up to 6 h (NSE >= 0.75). — [preprint](https://authorea.com/doi/full/10.22541/au.160396406.69787763)
- A 2024 study (in Chinese) argues that AR correction relies on the serial correlation of errors, which changes during rapid rises and near the peak, degrading the correction exactly there; it proposes an LSTM-based real-time corrector. — [Journal paper PDF](https://jour.hhu.edu.cn/szybh/article/pdf/20240618)
- Han & Morrison (2022, Journal of Hydrology) **[hourly, leads 1-18 h, Russian River]**: a sequence-to-sequence LSTM predicting the errors of National Water Model forecasts from observed precipitation and upstream gauge errors reduced percent bias from the range -60 to +80 % to -15 to +10 % and RMSE from 120 to 20 m^3/s; gains largest at short leads (abstract-level figures). — [record](https://agris.fao.org/search/fr/records/65df1aa34c5aef494fdfc779)
- Frame et al. (2020 AGU abstract) **[daily]**: an LSTM post-processor improved National Water Model NSE at 92 % of 531 basins. — [ADS abstract](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020AGUFMH121...06F/abstract)
- Weerts et al. (2011): quantile regression of past water-level forecast errors on the forecast value gives lead-time-dependent error quantiles from hindcasts. — [HESS 15, 255](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/15/255/2011/hess-15-255-2011.pdf)
- Timing-error correction as a post-processor for neural-network river forecasts (Abrahart et al.). — [record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/timing-error-correction-procedure-applied-to-neural-network-rainfall-runoff-modelling/811997513912418305-97347)
- A referee of the 2026 hourly-LSTM preprint likens its real-time discharge update to the AR(1) post-processor (EnsPost) of the US National Weather Service ensemble system. — [EGUsphere 2026-4885](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2026/egusphere-2026-4885/)

### Inferences
- The existing model already anchors on the last observation (it predicts the change from it), so the part of AR error updating that corrects the *level* at issue time is built in. What is not in the inputs is the model's own recent forecast error — e.g. how wrong the 1 h, 2 h and 3 h forecasts issued in the last hours turned out. A small corrector (linear AR per lead, or the errors added as extra input channels in a second-stage model) that uses these is the direct analogue of the literature above; the published decay rates suggest any gain will be largest at 1-2 h and small at 6 h, and the 2024 argument predicts it will be weakest during rapid rises, i.e. exactly where the lag hurts.
- Static per-station bias correction: the overall bias of a model trained on per-station scaled changes should already be near zero in calm conditions; the documented bias is conditional (hurricanes, 34 stations, +4 cm). A conditional correction (by station group and regime) is possible in principle, but with two storms in 2024-2025 it would be fitted and tested on the same events; it should be derived from training-period storms (2010-2022) and only then checked on the test storms.
- Quantile mapping is designed for distribution mismatch of simulations; for a forecast anchored on the last observation it has no obvious role, and no short-lead evidence for it was found.
- The Weerts-type quantile regression on hindcast errors doubles as an uncertainty estimate and is a simple alternative to the probabilistic heads of section 3.

### Gaps
- No study was found that applies AR / Kalman-type error updating on top of an LSTM that already uses real-time observations, at 1-6 h leads; expected gains are therefore inferred, not measured.
- No evidence on Kalman-filter-type recursive updating of LSTM stage forecasts was found.

## 9. Optimisation details with measured effect

### Takeaway
The measured effects of optimisation choices in hydrology LSTMs are small next to data scale: one wide layer (256 units) with strong dropout on the head, a step-down learning-rate schedule and per-site-normalised loss is the tested default; splitting a long look-back into a coarse long part and a fine recent part preserves accuracy at a fraction of the cost; multi-task gains are real for single-site models and mostly vanish in multi-site models.

### Cited Findings
- Kratzert et al. (2019) **[daily-sim]**: grid search over hidden size, dropout, sequence length and number of layers selected one LSTM layer, 256 cells, 270-day input sequence, dropout 0.4 on the head. — [HESS 23, 5089 (ar5iv copy)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1907.08456)
- Kratzert et al. (2024): regional model — hidden size 256, 40 % dropout, 30 epochs, learning rate 1e-3 reduced to 5e-4 at epoch 20 and 1e-4 at epoch 25, sequence length 365; single-basin models tuned over hidden size 8-32, head dropout 0-0.5, learning rate 1e-4 to 5e-3 and batch size 128-512 — small models are right only for small data. — [HESS 28, 4187](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/4187/2024/hess-28-4187-2024.html)
- Gauch, Mai & Lin (2021): the optimal input sequence length depends on the size of the training set (shorter is better with little data). — [arXiv 1911.07249](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.07249)
- Gauch et al. (2021) **[hourly]**: a naive hourly LSTM needs 4,320 input steps (180 d); the multi-timescale design uses 365 daily steps + 336 hourly steps with no loss in accuracy; loss = basin-normalised NSE averaged over timescales plus a consistency term between daily output and the mean of the 24 hourly outputs, which raised validation daily median NSE from 0.755 to 0.762; adding daily forcing products to the hourly branch raised hourly validation NSE from 0.776 to 0.812. — [HESS 25, 2045-2062](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/25/2045/2021/hess-25-2045-2021.html)
- Acuña Espinoza et al. (2025): a single LSTM cell processing several input frequencies cut processing time 5-fold relative to hourly-only training. — [HESS 29, 1749-1758](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/1749/2025/)
- Loss normalisation: MSE vs basin-normalised loss, mean NSE 0.66 -> 0.69, failing basins 6 -> 2 (Kratzert 2019, above). Station-weighted MSE with variance-based weights clipped to 0.2-5 (Mosaffa et al.). — [EGUsphere 2025-5008](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2025/egusphere-2025-5008/)
- Target/loss form in a multi-site setting: among 18 combinations of error power (absolute, squared, fourth power), weighting exponent and standardisation, none beat MSE on standardised targets significantly in the regional model; MAE-type losses helped single-site models over the whole range. — [HESS 30, 3623](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/30/3623/2026/hess-30-3623-2026.html)
- Multi-task learning: streamflow + stream temperature (Sadler et al. 2022, WRR) — the best multi-task model beat the single-task model at 56 of 101 sites when trained per site, but at only 17 sites significantly when one model was trained on all sites; streamflow + evapotranspiration on CAMELS (Ouyang et al. 2025, WRR) — median correlation 0.88 -> 0.90, with larger benefit when data are scarce. — [Sadler et al. record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1029/2021wr030138); [Eos highlight on Ouyang et al.](https://eos.org/editor-highlights/deep-learning-goes-multi-tasking)
- Fine-tuning hyper-parameters found by search per basin: 1-40 epochs, learning rate 1e-5 to 1e-3, loss chosen among NSE / MSE / RMSE (Ryd & Nearing). — [arXiv 2504.12559](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.12559)
- Consistency of multi-output training: in a multi-site peak study a multi-head variant gave no appreciable gain (Martel et al.). — [HESS 29, 4951](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/4951/2025/hess-29-4951-2025.html)

### Inferences
- The "no gain" results already obtained (third layer, longer look-back, per-type models, per-station initial state) agree with the literature default of one layer and with Kratzert 2024 on subsets. The one structural-training idea with measured benefit that has not been tried is the two-resolution look-back (e.g. several weeks at 6 h or daily resolution + 72 h hourly): it reaches slow states (river recession, antecedent wetness) without the cost and optimisation difficulty of a long hourly sequence. Since a longer hourly look-back did not help, the expected gain is modest and probably confined to large rivers and lakes.
- Width vs depth: the existing model uses 2 x 128; the benchmarked default is 1 x 256 with head dropout 0.4. This is a cheap comparison, but effect sizes in the literature are not reported separately, so no gain can be promised.
- A step-down learning-rate schedule in the last third of training (1e-3 -> 5e-4 -> 1e-4) is standard; combined with weight averaging over the low-rate phase it is the natural place to test SWA/EMA (section 7).
- Lead-specific loss weights: with a direct 6-lead head and a loss scaled by the 6 h-change std, the 1 h lead contributes little gradient. No source quantifies lead weighting; scaling each lead's error by that lead's own change std (the per-lead analogue of NSE*) is the principled variant to test.
- Auxiliary targets (maximum level in the next 6 h, time of maximum, sign of change) are multi-task additions; the Sadler result says gains shrink in multi-site models, so expectations should be low, but a "next-6 h maximum" head directly serves the peak use case and costs nothing at inference.
- Batch composition across stations (uniform over stations vs proportional to record length vs event-enriched) has no published measurement in this setting.

### Gaps
- No controlled study of dropout, weight decay, batch size or batch composition for hourly multi-gauge LSTMs with reported effect sizes was found.
- No study of lead-specific loss weights or of auxiliary targets such as the next-6 h maximum was found.
- No study jointly predicting stage and discharge with a comparison against single-target training was found (one preprint predicts both but does not report the comparison).

## 10. Evaluation practice: judging small gains

### Takeaway
The leading groups compare models site by site on paired scores (Wilcoxon signed-rank across basins), report an effect size next to the p-value because with hundreds of sites almost any difference is "significant", and average 8-10 seeds before comparing; differences below the seed-to-seed spread, or with Cohen's d below about 0.1, are described as practically equivalent.

### Cited Findings
- Kratzert et al. (2019): paired Wilcoxon test across 531 basins plus Cohen's d. LSTM with static inputs vs attribute-embedding LSTM: p = 1e-28 but d = 0.055 ("small effect"), mean NSE 0.69 vs 0.67. Against benchmark process models d = 0.22-0.45. Seed-to-seed std of mean NSE reported next to every single-model score (0.006-0.059). — [HESS 23, 5089 (ar5iv copy)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1907.08456)
- Gauch et al. (2025): one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank at alpha = 0.05 across basins for every pair of methods and missing-data level; differences with Cohen's d < 0.1 are called small even when significant; conclusion phrased as "tends to perform best, by a small margin". — [HESS 29, 6221-6235](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/6221/2025/)
- Kratzert et al. (2024) and Gauch et al. (2021): every configuration is an average of 10 seeds. — [HESS 28, 4187](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/28/4187/2024/hess-28-4187-2024.html); [HESS 25, 2045](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/25/2045/2021/hess-25-2045-2021.html)
- Ryd & Nearing (2025): 8 seeds x 159 basins = 1,272 paired comparisons; report the fraction of pairs made worse (22 %) and of basins made worse on average (14 %) next to the mean and median gain. — [arXiv 2504.12559](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.12559)
- A continental stream-temperature study tests each site separately (Wilcoxon signed-rank on absolute errors, alpha = 0.05) and labels sites as significantly better / no difference / significantly worse. — [arXiv 2410.19865](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.19865)
- Le Guen & Thome report every loss comparison as mean +- std over 10 training runs. — [arXiv 1909.09020](https://arxiv.org/html/1909.09020v4)
- An EGU 2026 abstract reports that not only seeds but also the computational backend (CUDA / PyTorch / library versions) changes LSTM trajectories; identical results require identical environment, seed and data. — [EGU26-1897](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-1897.html)
- Non-determinism alone moves the median NSE of the CAMELS benchmark by well below 0.01. — [arXiv 1911.07249 (ar5iv copy)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1911.07249)

### Inferences
- With a seed spread of +-0.04 cm on 8.4-8.7 cm (about 0.5 %), a candidate method should be compared as five seeds vs five seeds (or mean-of-five vs mean-of-five), paired by station: (a) Wilcoxon signed-rank on per-station RMSE differences, (b) Cohen's d or simply the median per-station change, (c) the fraction of stations that get worse, (d) a bootstrap over stations for the confidence interval of the overall RMSE change. The -2.8 % from ensembling is about 6 seed standard deviations and clearly real; a 0.5 % change is not distinguishable with one seed per arm.
- Hours within a station are strongly autocorrelated, so tests on individual hours overstate significance; resampling units should be stations and events (or blocks of several days).
- Hurricane RMSE rests on two storms. Whatever its size, a change there cannot be shown to be significant; storm results should be presented as case studies, and methods aimed at extremes should additionally be checked on the many smaller events (e.g. top 1 % of 6 h changes per station) where the sample is large, and on training-period storms held out by event.
- Model selection on 2023 validation RMSE alone will favour the smooth, late forecasts that squared error rewards; adding a rise-timing metric and an event-hours RMSE to the selection criteria is consistent with de Vos & Rientjes' multi-measure argument (section 1).

### Gaps
- No hydrology paper was found that studies bootstrap over events or block bootstrap for hourly forecast verification, or that gives guidance on the number of seeds needed to detect a given RMSE difference.
- No community benchmark exists for 1-6 h stage forecasting with observations as input, so effect sizes in this setting cannot be compared across papers.
